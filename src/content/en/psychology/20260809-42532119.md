## The key finding

A 2026 systematic review and meta-analysis examining 24 studies found that board games are linked to specific cognitive improvements depending on age. Researchers observed associations between board game play and enhanced verbal short-term memory in children, visuospatial short-term memory in kindergarteners, and processing speed in older adults. The analysis drew from 5,288 initially identified studies across four major scientific databases, with 16 studies meeting criteria for statistical pooling.

## What the study looked like

Researchers conducted a systematic review by searching Scopus, Web of Science, PsycINFO, and Medline databases for studies examining board games and executive functions—the mental processes that help us plan, focus attention, remember instructions, and juggle multiple tasks. From 5,288 potential studies, 24 met inclusion criteria for qualitative review, and 16 provided sufficient data for meta-analysis, which combines results across multiple studies to detect patterns. The included studies spanned different age groups from kindergarten children through older adults. The research team assessed study quality using risk-of-bias tools and performed meta-regression analysis to account for variations in study design. Participants across studies engaged in various board games, and researchers measured different aspects of executive function including working memory, processing speed, and other cognitive abilities.

## Why researchers think this happened

Board games inherently require players to use multiple cognitive processes simultaneously. When children play games, they must remember rules, plan moves ahead, and adapt strategies—all exercises for verbal and visuospatial memory systems. The findings align with existing theories that cognitive skills improve through practice in engaging, socially interactive contexts. For older adults, the processing speed improvements may reflect the demanding nature of gameplay that requires quick decision-making and response to changing game states. The social aspect of board games may also contribute, as interaction with others during play creates a richer cognitive environment than solitary activities. The authors note their findings connect to prior work suggesting that cognitively stimulating leisure activities correlate with better cognitive outcomes, though they emphasize the need to distinguish correlation from causation.

## How to read this carefully

The authors explicitly state that current evidence is not yet sufficient to strongly recommend board games as cognitive interventions. High heterogeneity appeared in some meta-analyses, meaning results varied considerably across studies—a red flag suggesting findings may not be consistent or generalizable. The review included only 16 studies in quantitative analysis, a relatively small evidence base for drawing firm conclusions. Many included studies may have had methodological limitations, and the review team conducted risk-of-bias assessments that revealed quality concerns. Crucially, these are associations, not proof of cause and effect. People who choose to play board games regularly might differ from non-players in unmeasured ways that could explain cognitive differences. The specific types of games, duration of play, and individual differences weren't fully explored across studies.

## What this means for everyday life

Given these findings, board games appear to be a low-risk activity that might support cognitive engagement across ages, though they shouldn't be viewed as guaranteed cognitive enhancers. For families, incorporating game nights could provide enjoyable social time that happens to involve mental exercise—the combination of fun and potential cognitive stimulation makes them appealing regardless of definitive proof. For older adults concerned about cognitive health, board games offer an accessible, social activity that correlates with maintained processing abilities, though they're no substitute for evidence-based interventions when cognitive decline is a concern. The key takeaway is modest optimism: board games are promising as one component of a cognitively active lifestyle, but more rigorous research is needed before making strong claims about their benefits.
