## The key finding

A 2025 systematic review and meta-analysis of 13 randomized controlled trials, involving 1,174 participants with an average age of 70.7 years, found that dance interventions were linked to modest improvements in cognitive function (effect size g = 0.27) and nearly moderate reductions in depression symptoms (g = 0.43). These benefits appeared regardless of the type of dance studied, the comparison group used, or other moderating factors researchers examined. The findings suggest that dance — which combines physical movement, cognitive challenge, and social interaction — may offer a multipronged approach to supporting brain health and mental well-being in older adults.

## What the study looked like

Researchers conducted a comprehensive search of five databases to identify randomized controlled trials testing dance interventions in older adults aged 60 and above. The 13 trials included in the meta-analysis enrolled healthy older adults or those living with mild cognitive impairment, with 66.3% of participants being female. The dance interventions varied widely, encompassing formal dance styles (like ballroom or contemporary) and traditional cultural dances. Comparison groups included usual activity, other forms of physical activity, or waitlist controls. Researchers extracted 76 individual effect sizes from these trials, which ranged from -0.97 to 2.88, and used robust statistical models to account for variation between studies. The analysis focused primarily on cognitive outcomes (such as memory, executive function, and processing speed) and depression symptoms, though only one trial examined anxiety and was excluded from pooled analysis.

## Why researchers think this happened

Dance is a unique form of physical activity that simultaneously engages multiple systems: it requires learning and remembering choreography (cognitive demand), coordinated movement and balance (physical challenge), and often involves partner work or group participation (social connection). The authors suggest that this multifaceted nature may explain why dance shows benefits for both cognition and mood. Physical activity alone is known to support brain health through improved blood flow and neuroplasticity, but dance adds layers of cognitive complexity — such as sequence learning, spatial navigation, and rhythm coordination — that may further stimulate neural networks. The social element may be particularly important for depression, as group dance settings foster connection and reduce isolation, both recognized risk factors for low mood in older populations. The study builds on prior research indicating that cognitively enriched physical activities may offer advantages over routine exercise alone.

## How to read this carefully

While the findings are promising, several limitations warrant caution. The effect sizes, though statistically significant, were small to moderate, meaning the real-world impact may be modest for any individual. The 13 trials varied considerably in dance style, session frequency, duration, and comparison groups, which could influence results in ways the moderator analysis didn't detect due to limited statistical power. Most participants were relatively healthy older adults, so it's unclear whether these benefits extend to those with more advanced cognitive decline or serious mental health conditions. Additionally, nearly all studies were short-term (typically weeks to months), leaving open questions about whether benefits persist with continued practice or fade after dance programs end. As with any meta-analysis, publication bias — the tendency for positive results to be published more often — could inflate the observed effects. This was an analysis of associations in controlled settings, not a definitive statement that dance causes cognitive or mood improvements.

## What this means for everyday life

Given these findings, older adults looking for engaging ways to stay active might consider dance as one option worth exploring. Dance classes offer a social environment that can be enjoyable and motivating, potentially making it easier to stick with compared to solitary exercise. The cognitive demands of learning steps and coordinating with music may provide additional brain stimulation beyond walking or cycling alone. For those concerned about mood or memory as they age, dance could be a low-risk activity to incorporate into a broader lifestyle approach that includes other evidence-based strategies like social connection, adequate sleep, and a balanced diet. It's important not to view dance as a standalone solution or guaranteed prevention for cognitive decline or depression, but rather as one potentially beneficial component of healthy aging. Individuals with mobility concerns or health conditions should consult a healthcare provider before starting any new exercise program, including dance.
