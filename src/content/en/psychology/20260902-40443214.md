## The key finding

A 2025 scoping review examining Indigenous contributions to food security in Canada analyzed 1,916 studies and identified 39 initiatives that center Indigenous perspectives in addressing hunger and sustainable food access. These programs—developed by or in partnership with Indigenous communities—demonstrate how traditional knowledge systems are being integrated with contemporary food strategies across mixed food environments. The review revealed that while Indigenous-informed approaches show promise for creating sustainable, community-centered solutions, Indigenous participation in formal decision-making processes at regional and national levels remains limited, pointing to a significant gap between on-the-ground innovation and policy influence.

## What the study looked like

Researchers conducted a systematic search across five online databases to map the landscape of Indigenous-informed food security work in Canada. From an initial pool of 1,916 results, they narrowed their analysis to 39 studies that met inclusion criteria—specifically examining initiatives, programs, policies, or strategies either developed by Indigenous communities, created in partnership with them, or explicitly centering Indigenous perspectives. The review focused on identifying key themes, gaps in current approaches, and recommendations for strengthening food security efforts. The included studies spanned various Canadian regions and examined how Indigenous groups navigate mixed food systems that combine traditional harvesting, hunting, and gathering practices with store-bought foods and contemporary agricultural methods.

## Why researchers think this happened

The authors propose that Indigenous-informed food security initiatives succeed because they integrate environmental stewardship, cultural preservation, and community knowledge accumulated over generations. Traditional foodways—the practices, relationships, and knowledge systems surrounding food—offer insights into sustainable resource management that complement modern approaches. The review suggests these initiatives work within mixed food systems because they acknowledge the reality of contemporary Indigenous life: communities draw on both traditional practices like fishing and hunting alongside grocery stores and farmers markets. However, the underrepresentation of Indigenous voices in broader decision-making likely stems from systemic barriers including colonial legacy, insufficient government consultation mechanisms, and a historical devaluation of traditional knowledge within formal policy structures. The researchers note that existing successful programs often emerged despite, rather than because of, institutional support.

## How to read this carefully

This scoping review maps existing literature rather than measuring outcomes, so it cannot tell us which specific interventions are most effective at reducing food insecurity. The 39 retained studies represent what has been published and documented, but many community-led initiatives may operate without academic documentation, meaning the true scope of Indigenous food security work likely exceeds what appears in databases. The review also cannot establish causal relationships—it shows correlation between Indigenous involvement and certain program characteristics, but doesn't prove that Indigenous leadership directly causes better outcomes. Additionally, "Indigenous communities" encompasses diverse nations, languages, and food traditions across Canada; what works in coastal communities may differ from prairie or Arctic contexts. Readers should avoid assuming a one-size-fits-all approach.

## What this means for everyday life

This research suggests that addressing food security effectively may require rethinking who holds expertise. For non-Indigenous Canadians involved in food policy, community food programs, or local agriculture, the findings point toward the value of collaborative models that genuinely share decision-making power rather than merely consulting Indigenous communities. The emphasis on cross-sectoral approaches—bringing together health, environment, education, and economic development—offers a framework anyone working on community resilience might consider. For Indigenous communities already leading this work, the review validates the importance of self-determination in food systems and highlights the need for institutional recognition of traditional knowledge as equally valid alongside scientific approaches. The integration of environmental stewardship with food security also speaks to broader conversations about climate adaptation and sustainable living that affect all communities facing environmental change.
