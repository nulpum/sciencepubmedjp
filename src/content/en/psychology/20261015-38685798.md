## The key finding

Researchers screened 1,063 studies on shared decision-making in wound care and found just 8 that met quality standards. Those interventions—focused on helping patients set goals and share knowledge with clinicians—were generally well-received and appeared to lower short-term decisional conflict. However, the evidence quality was rated as "very low" due to inconsistent outcome measures and highly variable intervention designs. This suggests a significant knowledge gap in how to effectively involve wound patients in treatment decisions.

## What the study looked like

This 2024 systematic review followed PRISMA 2020 guidelines and searched major databases including Medline, EMBASE, CINAHL, and trial registries through January 2023. Two independent reviewers screened abstracts and extracted data from interventional studies published in English that aimed to enhance shared decision-making in wound care or tissue viability. The review protocol was pre-registered with PROSPERO. Of the initial 1,063 abstracts identified, only 8 full-text studies ultimately qualified for inclusion—a remarkably small yield that itself highlights how sparse the research base is in this clinical area.

## Why researchers think this happened

The authors note that wound care is inherently complex: healing doesn't occur in isolation but interacts with patient symptoms, psychological wellbeing, and treatment effectiveness. Despite growing clinical emphasis on patient-centered care and personalization in wound management, research on shared decision-making interventions has lagged behind. The inconsistency in reported outcomes across the 8 included studies suggests researchers haven't yet agreed on which metrics matter most—whether decisional conflict, treatment adherence, satisfaction, or healing rates. The variation in intervention components—some combining goal-setting with educational tools, others using question prompts—reflects experimentation rather than a settled evidence base. The authors suggest future work should involve stakeholders (patients and clinicians) from the outset and develop program theories that acknowledge intervention complexity.

## How to read this carefully

The "very low certainty" rating is crucial context. This doesn't mean the interventions don't work—it means we can't draw firm conclusions from such a small, heterogeneous set of studies. Publication bias is possible: interventions that didn't improve outcomes may not have been published. The review also included only English-language studies, potentially excluding relevant international research. Because wound types vary widely (surgical wounds, pressure ulcers, diabetic foot ulcers), interventions effective for one population may not translate to another. Finally, "short-term decisional conflict" is just one outcome; whether these interventions improve long-term healing, quality of life, or cost-effectiveness remains unknown.

## What this means for everyday life

If you or a loved one is managing a chronic wound, this review suggests asking your care team about setting personal goals together—what matters most to you in treatment. The evidence hints that collaborative goal-setting and having space to ask questions may reduce the uncertainty you feel about treatment options, even if it doesn't yet guarantee better physical outcomes. Given the research gaps, it's worth advocating for yourself: request explanations in plain language, voice your priorities (like reducing pain versus faster healing), and ask how decisions align with your daily life. Clinicians should note that structured approaches to elicit patient needs appear promising but need further validation before being adopted as standard practice.
