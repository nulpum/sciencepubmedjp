## The key finding

A 2026 scoping review analyzing eight studies on interprofessional education (IPE) for medical students found a paradox: while students reported positive attitudes toward collaborative practice and appreciated learning with peers from other healthcare disciplines, they showed inconsistent understanding of what different professionals actually do. The review identified three major barriers—unclear professional roles, hierarchical dynamics between professions, and time and organizational constraints—alongside three key enablers: curriculum integration, practice-based learning, and interactive support structures. The findings suggest that simply bringing students together isn't enough; programs must actively address role clarification to make interprofessional training effective.

## What the study looked like

Researchers conducted a scoping review following established Arksey and O'Malley guidelines and reported results according to PRISMA-ScR extension standards. They searched three major electronic databases (PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science) for studies published between 2014 and 2024 that examined medical students' experiences with interprofessional education. From the initial search results, eight studies met the inclusion criteria for final analysis. The review team synthesized findings across these studies to identify patterns in student experiences, challenges they encountered, and factors that enabled successful interprofessional learning. The methodology allowed researchers to map the current landscape of IPE implementation rather than evaluating effectiveness through controlled trials.

## Why researchers think this happened

The review suggests that the disconnect between positive attitudes and unclear role understanding may stem from how IPE programs are designed and delivered. Medical education has traditionally emphasized profession-specific training, creating siloed learning environments where students gain limited exposure to what other healthcare workers contribute to patient care. When interprofessional activities are added without explicit attention to role clarification, students may enjoy the collaborative experience without developing functional knowledge of team dynamics. The hierarchical nature of healthcare professions—with physicians often positioned atop traditional structures—may further complicate students' ability to recognize the expertise other professionals bring. Time pressures in medical curricula and organizational barriers to coordinating across programs likely prevent the sustained interaction needed for genuine understanding of professional boundaries and collaborative workflows.

## How to read this carefully

This scoping review synthesized only eight studies, a relatively small evidence base that limits generalizability. Scoping reviews map existing research rather than assess quality or synthesize effect sizes, so we cannot determine which IPE approaches work best or quantify their impact. The included studies likely varied in how they defined and measured "understanding of professional roles," making direct comparisons difficult. Publication bias may mean that programs with negative outcomes are underrepresented in the literature. Additionally, self-reported attitudes don't necessarily predict actual collaborative behavior in clinical practice. The review focused on medical students specifically, so findings may not apply to other health professions students or to practicing clinicians. The 2014-2024 timeframe captures recent work but may miss earlier foundational studies.

## What this means for everyday life

For patients, this review highlights why healthcare teams sometimes struggle with coordination—if medical students graduate without clearly understanding what nurses, pharmacists, physical therapists, and social workers do, communication breakdowns become more likely in clinical settings. If you've experienced fragmented care where providers seem unaware of each other's contributions, inadequate interprofessional training during education may be a contributing factor. For medical and health professions students, the findings suggest it's worth seeking out collaborative learning opportunities that go beyond surface-level interaction. Ask questions about other professionals' training, scope of practice, and decision-making processes. For educators developing IPE programs, the review indicates that simply scheduling joint sessions isn't sufficient—explicit role clarification activities and practice-based learning appear essential for preparing students to function effectively in team-based care environments.
