## The key finding

A scoping review of nine U.S.-based studies found that exam wrappers—structured reflection tools completed after assessments—are associated with enhanced metacognitive skills, improved study behaviors, and better academic performance among undergraduate nursing students. These tools guide students through post-exam reflection using formats ranging from written grids to one-on-one debriefs with faculty. Researchers identified three key outcomes: variations in how exam wrappers are delivered, measurable improvements in learning and metacognitive awareness, and creation of academic support opportunities between students and instructors.

## What the study looked like

This 2025 scoping review followed a five-stage framework developed by Arksey and O'Malley. Researchers searched nine academic databases in August 2024, with an update in May 2025, specifically looking for studies involving undergraduate nursing students and exam wrapper interventions. All nine studies meeting inclusion criteria were conducted in U.S. nursing programs. The review used narrative synthesis to analyze findings and organized results thematically. Rather than testing a single hypothesis, this scoping review mapped the existing research landscape to identify patterns, gaps, and implementation approaches across different educational settings. The studies examined varied in their specific designs, participant numbers, and wrapper formats, but all focused on how these reflection tools influenced nursing students' learning processes and metacognitive development.

## Why researchers think this happened

Metacognition—essentially "thinking about thinking"—is considered essential for clinical reasoning and self-regulated learning in nursing, where practitioners must constantly evaluate their own decision-making processes in high-stakes situations. Exam wrappers appear to work by creating structured moments for students to analyze their test preparation strategies, identify knowledge gaps, and adjust future study behaviors. The researchers noted that while exam wrappers have demonstrated success in other academic disciplines, their adoption in nursing education has been limited and inconsistent. The review suggests that when students actively reflect on what study methods worked or failed, they develop stronger awareness of their own learning processes. Faculty involvement emerged as crucial—the quality of feedback and availability of instructor time influenced how effectively students engaged with the reflection process and translated insights into behavioral changes.

## How to read this carefully

Several limitations warrant caution when interpreting these findings. All nine included studies came from U.S. institutions, raising questions about whether results would generalize to nursing programs in other countries with different educational cultures or healthcare systems. The review did not specify sample sizes across studies or distinguish between different types of research designs, making it difficult to assess the strength of evidence. Additionally, the studies examined short-term outcomes like immediate study behavior changes and course performance, but did not track whether metacognitive improvements persisted into clinical practice or advanced coursework. The review identified that faculty time and institutional support influenced implementation success, suggesting exam wrappers may work differently in resource-constrained settings. Correlation between exam wrapper use and improved outcomes does not necessarily mean the wrappers caused the improvements—motivated students might engage more thoroughly with any reflective tool.

## What this means for everyday life

For nursing students and educators, this research suggests that structured post-exam reflection might offer practical benefits beyond simply reviewing correct answers. If you're a nursing student, consider creating your own post-test analysis routine: after each exam, spend 15 minutes writing down which study methods helped most, where you felt unprepared, and what you'll adjust next time. Given the findings linking exam wrappers to improved study behaviors, this habit might help you develop the self-monitoring skills essential for clinical settings where you'll need to evaluate your own clinical reasoning in real time. For nursing educators, the review indicates that digital platforms and early curriculum integration may improve adoption rates—though faculty time investment remains a practical consideration. The researchers emphasize that feedback literacy matters: simply completing a reflection form is less valuable than receiving and applying constructive guidance on how to improve learning strategies.
