## The key finding

A 2024 narrative review found that tooth wear appears to be more severe in people with Parkinson's disease (PD) compared to healthy controls, based on analysis of just four published studies — one case report and three questionnaire-based surveys. The review also identified potential links between tooth wear and bruxism (teeth grinding), temporomandibular disorder (TMD) pain, and lower oral health-related quality of life in PD patients. The scarcity of research on this topic — only four relevant publications — highlights a major gap in understanding how neurodegenerative disease affects oral health.

## What the study looked like

Researchers conducted a literature search in PubMed to identify all published studies examining tooth wear in Parkinson's disease patients. They found only four publications: one case report describing a single patient and three studies that used questionnaires to assess oral health and tooth wear. None of the studies involved direct clinical examination of tooth surfaces or standardized wear measurements. The questionnaire-based studies compared self-reported or clinician-reported tooth wear between PD patients and healthy controls, and explored associations with bruxism, TMD pain, and oral health quality of life. The review did not perform statistical pooling due to the small number and heterogeneous design of the studies.

## Why researchers think this happened

The review authors propose that multiple Parkinson's-related factors may converge to accelerate tooth wear. Bruxism — involuntary clenching and grinding of teeth — is known to be more common in PD patients, possibly due to dopaminergic medication side effects or motor control changes in the jaw muscles. TMD pain, which often coexists with bruxism, may also reflect altered muscle tone or dystonia in the masticatory system. Additionally, reduced saliva flow (a common issue in PD) may diminish the protective buffering effect of saliva, making tooth enamel more vulnerable to mechanical wear. The authors hypothesize that risk factors for tooth wear seen in the general population — such as acidic diet, grinding habits, and poor oral hygiene — may be more prevalent or more severe in people with Parkinson's, creating a compounding effect.

## How to read this carefully

This review is based on extremely limited evidence: only four studies, none of which used standardized clinical measures of tooth wear. Three of the four relied on questionnaires, which are subject to recall bias and cannot objectively quantify wear severity. The single case report, while illustrative, cannot establish prevalence or causation. No large-scale cohort studies or randomized trials exist. The review's conclusions are explicitly framed as hypotheses rather than established facts. Readers should be cautious about generalizing these findings until larger, clinically validated studies are conducted. The association between PD and tooth wear has not been rigorously tested, and alternative explanations (such as age, medication, or comorbidities) have not been ruled out.

## What this means for everyday life

If you or a loved one has Parkinson's disease, it may be worth discussing tooth wear and jaw habits with a dentist familiar with neurodegenerative conditions. Signs to watch for include flattened tooth surfaces, increased sensitivity, jaw pain, or a history of grinding. While this review does not prove that PD directly causes tooth wear, the potential link suggests that proactive dental monitoring could help catch problems early. Custom night guards to reduce grinding, saliva substitutes for dry mouth, and regular dental exams may help protect teeth. Given the overlap between bruxism, TMD, and tooth wear, a multidisciplinary approach involving neurology, dentistry, and physical therapy might offer the best support for oral health in Parkinson's patients.
