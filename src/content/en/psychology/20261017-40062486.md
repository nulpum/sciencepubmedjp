## The key finding

A 2025 systematic review of 44 studies spanning five decades found that only 7 of 21 established working memory benchmarks have been confirmed in olfaction, while 2 failed to generalize and 12 remain insufficiently tested. This means that the foundational principles psychologists use to explain how we hold information in mind—largely built on vision and hearing research—may not fully capture how memory works across all human senses. The findings suggest that working memory theory has been constructed on a narrow sensory foundation, potentially missing critical insights about how the brain processes and retains different types of information.

## What the study looked like

Researchers conducted a systematic review of all peer-reviewed studies published over the past 50 years that tested working memory using smell-based tasks. They identified 44 qualifying studies and evaluated whether each of 21 widely accepted working memory benchmarks—standards like capacity limits, decay over time, interference effects, and associations with other cognitive abilities—held true when participants were asked to remember odors instead of sights or sounds. The team categorized each benchmark as generalized (replicated in olfaction), failed (contradicted by olfactory evidence), mixed (inconsistent findings), or unaddressed (no studies available). This approach allowed them to map which core assumptions about working memory survive when tested outside the audio-visual domain that dominates cognitive psychology.

## Why researchers think this happened

The authors argue that the historical focus on vision and hearing in working memory research reflects practical convenience and lab tradition rather than theoretical justification. Visual and auditory stimuli are easier to control, present, and measure in laboratory settings, which has led generations of researchers to build models based almost exclusively on these senses. The review highlights that some working memory principles—like basic capacity limits and short-term retention—do transfer to smell, suggesting a common underlying architecture. However, exceptions emerged in how olfactory working memory correlates with other cognitive functions, hinting that the sense of smell may recruit partially distinct neural mechanisms or follow different processing rules. The authors suggest these gaps reveal that existing frameworks may be incomplete, built on implicit assumptions that don't hold universally across sensory modalities.

## How to read this carefully

This is a review of existing studies, not new experimental data, so its conclusions are constrained by the available literature. The fact that 12 benchmarks remain insufficiently tested means absence of evidence, not evidence of absence—future research may confirm or refute these principles in olfaction. The studies reviewed varied widely in methodology, sample sizes, and odor stimuli used, making direct comparisons difficult. Additionally, olfactory research faces unique challenges: odors are harder to standardize, individual differences in smell sensitivity are large, and verbal labels for smells can confound purely sensory memory. The review also doesn't address whether similar gaps exist for touch, taste, or other underexplored senses, so the problem may be even broader than the authors document.

## What this means for everyday life

If you've ever wondered why a scent can trigger vivid memories differently than a photo or song, this research hints at why: the memory systems handling smell may follow partly different rules than those governing what you see and hear. For anyone interested in how memory works—whether you're a student, educator, or someone exploring cognitive strategies—this review suggests that popular frameworks based on vision and hearing might not tell the whole story. It's a reminder that scientific models are only as robust as the evidence base supporting them, and that exploring neglected areas like olfactory memory could reveal new ways to understand attention, learning, and even conditions like dementia, where smell loss often appears early. Given this, it might be worth considering that your "mind's nose" operates with its own logic, one that psychology is only beginning to map.
