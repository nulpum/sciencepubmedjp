## The key finding

A 2025 review of 40 physical activity intervention studies revealed a striking gap: while researchers frequently invoke "empowerment" as a strategy to help people become more active, none of the studies explicitly defined what exercise empowerment actually means, and none directly measured it. Despite this conceptual void, 72.5% of the interventions (29 out of 40 studies) did report increases in physical activity. Only 22.5% of studies used empowerment theory to guide their approach, and 15% had no theoretical foundation at all. The review analyzed randomized controlled trials and quasi-experimental studies published since 1994, revealing a disconnect between empowerment as a buzzword and empowerment as a rigorous framework.

## What the study looked like

Researchers conducted a scoping review, searching five major databases (PubMed, Embase, PsycINFO, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, and CINAHL) for studies involving both adults and children. They included only randomized controlled trials and quasi-experimental studies published in English since 1994 that described physical activity interventions claiming to use empowerment strategies. After screening, 40 papers met the criteria. The reviewers examined how each study defined empowerment, whether they measured it, what theoretical frameworks guided the interventions, what strategies were used, and whether physical activity levels changed. The majority of interventions (55%) relied on educational approaches. Physical activity was measured subjectively—often through self-reported questionnaires rather than objective tracking devices—in 67.5% of the studies.

## Why researchers think this happened

The review authors propose that the vagueness around exercise empowerment reflects the field's failure to translate empowerment theory into practical intervention design. Empowerment is a multidimensional concept involving personal confidence, social support, and access to community resources, but interventions have largely reduced it to simple education. Researchers may invoke "empowerment" because it sounds participatory and person-centered, aligning with contemporary health promotion values, without rigorously operationalizing what that means in an exercise context. The authors note that when theoretical frameworks were used, they often came from general empowerment theory rather than being adapted specifically for physical activity. This suggests that while the concept of empowerment resonates intuitively with researchers, the field lacks consensus on how to define, measure, and cultivate it in exercise settings.

## How to read this carefully

This is a scoping review, meaning it maps what exists in the literature rather than pooling data to estimate effect sizes. The finding that 72.5% of studies reported increases in physical activity doesn't prove empowerment strategies work—it simply shows that interventions using the word "empowerment" often see improvements, which could result from other factors like social support, accountability, or the Hawthorne effect. Since most studies measured physical activity subjectively through self-reports, these increases could reflect reporting bias rather than genuine behavior change. The review also doesn't tell us whether interventions that better aligned with empowerment theory were more effective than those that didn't. The included studies spanned diverse populations and contexts, making it difficult to generalize about what works for whom.

## What this means for everyday life

If you've joined exercise programs that promise to "empower" you, this review suggests you should ask what that actually means in practice. The research indicates that effective physical activity support likely involves more than just education—it requires building your confidence in making exercise choices, connecting you with supportive communities, and ensuring you have access to resources like safe walking paths or affordable fitness options. When evaluating a new fitness program, look for concrete elements: Does it help you identify exercises you genuinely enjoy? Does it connect you with others pursuing similar goals? Does it acknowledge barriers like time, cost, or access and help you navigate them? The review suggests that sustained physical activity may depend less on willpower and more on cultivating a sense of control and fulfillment in your exercise choices, supported by your social environment and community resources.
