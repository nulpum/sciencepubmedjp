## The key finding

A 2024 systematic review analyzing 11 studies with 1,090 nursing and midwifery students found that immersive simulation training was linked to measurably better outcomes than traditional teaching. Among studies measuring engagement, 80% (4 out of 5) observed higher post-test engagement scores in students who used immersive simulation. For motivation, 83% of studies (5 out of 6) found higher motivational scores in the immersive groups. Most notably, among 8 studies evaluating academic performance, 5 reported statistically significant improvements (P<0.001) favoring students who trained with immersive simulation compared to conventional teaching approaches.

## What the study looked like

Researchers conducted a systematic review following rigorous PROSPERO registry protocols, searching four major academic databases: Scopus, PubMed, Web of Science, and Science Direct. They screened 90 initially identified studies and selected 11 that met quality standards using the Medical Education Research Study Quality Instrument. The included studies collectively examined 1,090 nursing and midwifery students across various educational settings. These studies compared immersive simulation—technology-driven environments such as virtual reality scenarios that replicate clinical situations—against traditional teaching methods like lectures, textbook learning, or standard laboratory practice. The researchers specifically looked at three outcome measures: student engagement (how actively students participated), motivation (their drive to learn), and academic performance (test scores and competency assessments).

## Why researchers think this happened

The paper suggests that immersive simulation may enhance learning outcomes through several mechanisms grounded in educational theory. These technology-based environments allow students to practice clinical skills in safe, controlled settings where mistakes don't harm real patients, potentially reducing anxiety and increasing willingness to experiment with techniques. The immersive nature—where students feel present in realistic scenarios—may activate deeper cognitive processing compared to passive learning from lectures or textbooks. Additionally, the interactive quality of simulation requires active decision-making and problem-solving in real-time, which aligns with experiential learning principles. The review authors note that previous research in health education has pointed to the importance of hands-on, contextual learning, and immersive simulation appears to amplify these benefits by providing repeatable, standardized scenarios that would be difficult to guarantee in traditional clinical rotations.

## How to read this carefully

Several limitations should temper interpretation of these findings. The review included only 11 studies with 1,090 total participants—a relatively modest evidence base for drawing broad conclusions about an entire educational approach. The studies varied in how they defined and measured "immersive simulation," which may have included different technologies from basic computer simulations to full virtual reality systems, making direct comparisons challenging. Not all studies measured all three outcomes, and the review doesn't specify whether improvements translated to better patient care in actual clinical settings after graduation. Most importantly, this evidence shows associations and correlations, not proof that immersive simulation causes better learning outcomes—other factors like student enthusiasm for new technology, instructor expertise, or additional practice time might explain some differences.

## What this means for everyday life

For nursing students and educators, these findings suggest immersive simulation might be worth exploring as a complement to traditional training, though it shouldn't necessarily replace hands-on clinical experience entirely. Students considering nursing programs might ask about technology-enhanced learning opportunities, while recognizing that quality instruction matters more than flashy technology alone. For parents of healthcare students or anyone concerned about healthcare training quality, this research indicates the field is actively investigating better preparation methods. The broader takeaway extends beyond nursing: when learning complex skills—whether medical procedures, emergency responses, or technical trades—practicing in realistic but safe environments where mistakes become learning opportunities may enhance both confidence and competence compared to learning exclusively from books or lectures.
