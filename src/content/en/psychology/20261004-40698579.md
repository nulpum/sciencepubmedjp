## The key finding

A 2025 scoping review of sleep assessment tools in pediatric oncology reveals that no single method adequately addresses the full spectrum of sleep disturbances these patients face. While actigraphy and sleep diaries are widely used for their convenience, they show inconsistencies in measurement and interpretation. Validated sleep scales vary in psychometric strength across age groups and treatment stages. Polysomnography—the gold standard—proves too complex and costly for routine clinical use. Across all tools, researchers found a troubling gap: measured parameters often fail to align with the clinically relevant sleep problems that matter most to young cancer patients.

## What the study looked like

Researchers conducted a scoping review, systematically identifying and analyzing studies that used sleep assessment tools in pediatric cancer populations. They examined four main categories of tools: actigraphy (wrist-worn devices tracking movement), sleep diaries (patient or parent self-reports), validated sleep scales (standardized questionnaires), and polysomnography (comprehensive overnight sleep lab testing). For each tool type, the review team evaluated general reliability—how consistently the tool measures sleep—feasibility in both clinical and research settings, and how well the tool addresses concerns specific to children undergoing cancer treatment. The analysis considered how these tools performed across different age ranges and treatment phases, from initial diagnosis through active therapy.

## Why researchers think this happened

Pediatric cancer patients experience sleep disruptions from multiple, overlapping sources: the disease itself, intensive treatment regimens including chemotherapy and radiation, and the hospital environment with its frequent nighttime interruptions for vital signs and medication. This creates a complexity that no single assessment tool was designed to capture. Actigraphy and diaries, while noninvasive and practical, may miss subjective sleep quality issues or struggle with young children who cannot self-report accurately. Sleep scales developed for general pediatric populations may not account for cancer-specific concerns like pain from procedures or anxiety about treatment. Polysomnography captures detailed physiological data but requires specialized equipment, trained technicians, and an overnight stay—barriers that are particularly challenging for immunocompromised patients or those mid-treatment. The mismatch between what tools measure and what clinically matters reflects the absence of oncology-specific validation for most instruments.

## How to read this carefully

This review synthesizes existing tools rather than testing them head-to-head in a controlled study, so it cannot definitively rank which method works best for specific situations. The findings reflect patterns across multiple studies with varying designs, patient populations, and treatment protocols—meaning results may not apply uniformly to every pediatric cancer center or patient subgroup. The review identifies gaps but does not present new data on patient outcomes linked to specific assessment choices. Clinicians should recognize that "best" tool selection depends heavily on local context: a well-resourced research hospital might feasibly use polysomnography, while a community oncology clinic might rely more practically on parent-reported diaries. The variability in tool performance across age groups means findings from adolescent studies may not transfer to toddlers.

## What this means for everyday life

For families navigating pediatric cancer, this review underscores that sleep problems deserve systematic attention alongside more visible treatment side effects. If your child's care team uses sleep diaries or questionnaires, understanding their limitations can help you advocate for additional assessment if sleep issues persist despite normal scores. Parents might consider tracking multiple dimensions—total sleep time, nighttime awakenings, daytime fatigue, and emotional responses to sleep disruption—since single-measure tools may miss part of the picture. For clinicians and researchers, the review suggests a tailored approach: matching tool choice to the specific sleep concern (sleep duration versus quality versus circadian disruption), the child's developmental stage, and whether the goal is clinical monitoring or research data collection. Given this complexity, it might be worth considering combinations of methods—pairing objective actigraphy with subjective parent reports, for example—to build a more complete understanding of how cancer treatment affects a child's rest.
