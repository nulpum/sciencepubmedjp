## The key finding

A 2024 systematic review analyzing 33 studies found that individuals with eating disorders consistently showed poorer oral health across multiple measures compared to people without these conditions. Those with eating disorders had higher rates of dental erosion, cavities, gum inflammation, jaw pain, tooth sensitivity, and oral lesions. They also showed lower salivary flow and more acidic saliva—both risk factors that create conditions for tooth decay and enamel damage. The findings suggest dental professionals may be uniquely positioned to identify early signs of eating disorders during routine appointments.

## What the study looked like

Researchers conducted a comprehensive search across five medical databases, ultimately reviewing 33 studies that compared oral health outcomes between people diagnosed with eating disorders and healthy control groups. The review examined a wide range of oral health markers: dental caries (cavities), tooth erosion, gum health, saliva quality and quantity, jaw pain, teeth grinding, bite alignment, oral lesions, tooth sensitivity, and oral health-related quality of life. The research team followed rigorous systematic review protocols, with two independent reviewers screening studies, extracting data, and assessing study quality using established GRADE criteria to evaluate the certainty of evidence.

## Why researchers think this happened

The mechanisms connecting eating disorders to oral health problems are multifaceted. Purging behaviors expose teeth to stomach acid, which directly erodes enamel—the protective outer layer of teeth. Restrictive eating patterns often lead to nutritional deficiencies that compromise tissue health throughout the body, including gums and oral mucosa. The review found that people with eating disorders had reduced salivary flow and lower (more acidic) saliva pH, which matters because saliva normally neutralizes acids and helps remineralize teeth. Without adequate saliva production, the mouth's natural defense system weakens. Additionally, psychological stress associated with eating disorders may contribute to jaw clenching and other oral behaviors. The constellation of these factors creates an environment where multiple oral health problems can develop simultaneously.

## How to read this carefully

While the pattern across studies was consistent, the researchers noted that the certainty of evidence was "very low" using GRADE criteria, meaning the effect size estimates remain uncertain. This doesn't mean the findings are wrong—rather, that the existing studies varied in design, sample size, and measurement methods, making precise quantification difficult. The review included various types of eating disorders (anorexia nervosa, bulimia nervosa, and others), and outcomes varied by subtype in ways not fully parsed in this analysis. Most included studies were observational rather than randomized trials, which means we're seeing associations rather than proven causal relationships. The directionality question remains partially open: do eating disorders cause oral problems, or might pre-existing oral issues contribute to disordered eating in some cases?

## What this means for everyday life

This research highlights an often-overlooked aspect of eating disorders: their visible impact on oral health. For individuals struggling with disordered eating, understanding this connection might prompt earlier conversations with healthcare providers—dentists included. The findings suggest that dental professionals, who see patients more frequently than many other healthcare providers, could serve as an early detection point. If you notice unexplained tooth erosion, persistent sensitivity, or recurrent cavities despite good hygiene habits, it might be worth discussing broader health patterns with your dentist in a confidential setting. For parents and loved ones, oral health changes could be one observable indicator among others. The research underscores that eating disorders affect the whole body in interconnected ways, and that addressing them benefits not just mental health and nutrition, but physical health markers we encounter daily—like being able to eat without pain or smile without self-consciousness.
