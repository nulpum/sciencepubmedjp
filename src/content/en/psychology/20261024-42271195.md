## The key finding

A 2026 qualitative systematic review of 15 studies across international healthcare education reveals that entrustment—the process by which learners are granted graded autonomy in clinical settings—functions most effectively as a relational, co-constructed practice shaped by trust, context, and dialogue. The review found that feedback is the central mechanism through which learners make sense of supervision levels and develop professionally. Without high-quality dialogic feedback, entrustment risks devolving into a procedural checklist rather than a meaningful educational process. The findings challenge overly technical interpretations of entrustment and suggest that assessment approaches should prioritise narrative feedback and contextual awareness over standardised tick-boxes.

## What the study looked like

The researchers conducted a qualitative systematic review using thematic synthesis, analyzing 15 qualitative and mixed-methods studies that explored how entrustment is understood, experienced, and enacted in pre-registration practice-based learning (PBL) across healthcare professions internationally. The review specifically examined implications for UK dietetics education, which is implementing a national common assessment tool. Studies included focused on how entrustment shapes learner participation, supervision quality, and capability development in real-world clinical settings. The synthesis identified recurring themes around the relational nature of entrustment, the role of feedback, and how workplace pressures and local contextual factors influence decisions about granting learners autonomy.

## Why researchers think this happened

The review authors argue that entrustment creates what they term a 'credible developmental space'—a zone where learners can engage in authentic workplace activities with supported autonomy. This space is inherently relational, shaped by factors including trust between supervisor and learner, familiarity built over time, perceived risk in the clinical situation, and contextual constraints such as workload or staffing. The researchers propose that entrustment decisions are co-constructed through ongoing negotiation between learner and supervisor, rather than being unilaterally determined by the supervisor or dictated by a standardised checklist. They suggest that when feedback is dialogic and narrative-rich, it helps learners understand expectations, interpret their performance, and develop professional identity—functions that cannot be captured by simple yes/no judgments or competency checklists.

## How to read this carefully

This was a qualitative systematic review, meaning it synthesised themes across existing studies rather than generating new empirical data. The 15 included studies varied in healthcare profession, country, and setting, which strengthens generalisability but also means findings may not apply equally to all contexts. The review focused specifically on pre-registration (student-level) training, so findings may not fully translate to postgraduate or continuing professional development settings. Additionally, because entrustment is shaped by local workplace culture and resources, implementing these insights requires adaptation to specific institutional contexts rather than a one-size-fits-all approach. The review does not provide evidence that entrustment-based approaches improve patient outcomes or learner competence compared to traditional methods—it explores how entrustment is experienced and enacted, not its measurable effectiveness.

## What this means for everyday life

For healthcare students and educators, this review suggests that meaningful professional development in clinical placements depends less on checking off competency lists and more on building trusting relationships and engaging in rich, ongoing conversations about performance. Students might benefit from seeking out supervisors who provide narrative feedback—explanations of why they're being granted more or less autonomy, what they're doing well, and where growth is needed—rather than just numerical ratings. For educators and placement coordinators, the findings suggest that a small number of meaningful tasks with succinct narrative feedback may be more developmentally valuable than exhaustive checklists. The review also implies that workplace pressures and staffing constraints genuinely affect learning opportunities, validating students' experiences when placements feel rushed or unsupportive. Given these insights, it may be worth considering how feedback conversations are structured in your own learning or teaching environment—whether they create space for shared sensemaking or simply confirm compliance with standards.
