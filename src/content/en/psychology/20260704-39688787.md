## The key finding

Digital technology interventions — computer-based brain training programs, virtual reality exercises, and similar digital tools — showed meaningful improvements across multiple cognitive domains in adults aged 60 and older. In a 2025 meta-analysis examining 23 randomized controlled trials with 1,454 participants, researchers found that these interventions were linked to improvements in global cognitive function (standardized mean difference of 0.479), attention and processing speed (SMD = 0.488), executive function (SMD = 0.287), immediate recall (SMD = 0.266), and working memory (SMD = 0.307). The effects were particularly pronounced among participants who already had some cognitive impairment.

## What the study looked like

This systematic review analyzed randomized controlled trials — the gold standard for clinical research — published through May 2023. The research team searched five major medical databases for studies testing digital cognitive interventions in adults 60 years and older. All 23 included studies randomly assigned participants to either receive digital brain training or serve as controls, then measured various aspects of cognitive performance. The interventions varied widely: some used standard computerized cognitive tasks, others employed virtual reality environments, and some combined multiple approaches. Session frequency ranged from low to high intensity, with durations from brief 15-minute sessions to longer 60-minute programs. Some participants trained independently at home while others worked with professional guidance in specialized settings.

## Why researchers think this happened

The researchers propose that digital interventions work through neuroplasticity — the brain's ability to form new neural connections even in older age. Cognitive training may strengthen existing neural pathways and create new ones, particularly when exercises target specific mental skills repeatedly. Virtual reality-based interventions showed especially strong effects, possibly because immersive environments engage multiple sensory and cognitive systems simultaneously, creating richer learning experiences. The finding that cognitively impaired participants showed greater benefits aligns with the "magnification hypothesis" — those with more room for improvement may show larger gains. Professional guidance appeared beneficial, potentially because trainers can adjust difficulty levels, provide motivation, and ensure proper technique. Longer sessions (over 30 minutes) and extended programs (more than 24 sessions) likely allowed sufficient practice time for meaningful skill development and consolidation.

## How to read this carefully

Several limitations deserve consideration. The total sample size of 1,454 participants across 23 studies means individual trials were relatively small, averaging around 63 participants each. The studies showed considerable variation in intervention design, making it difficult to identify the single "best" approach. Most importantly, standardized mean differences in the 0.2–0.5 range represent small to medium effects — statistically significant but not transformative changes. We cannot determine from this analysis whether cognitive improvements translated into better daily functioning or reduced dementia risk long-term, as most studies measured outcomes immediately post-intervention. Publication bias is also a concern; studies showing no effect may be less likely to get published. Finally, this meta-analysis examined association, not causation in the strictest sense, though randomized trials provide stronger evidence than observational studies.

## What this means for everyday life

Given these findings, older adults interested in maintaining cognitive health might consider incorporating digital brain training into their routines, though expectations should remain realistic. The evidence suggests that structured, repeated practice with cognitive tasks — whether through specialized apps, computer programs, or virtual reality systems — may support mental sharpness across multiple domains. The research indicates that consistency matters: programs involving more than 24 sessions with individual sessions lasting over 30 minutes showed stronger associations with improvement. For those already experiencing mild cognitive difficulties, these interventions appeared particularly beneficial. However, digital training should complement, not replace, other evidence-based approaches to brain health including physical exercise, social engagement, and management of cardiovascular risk factors. Anyone concerned about cognitive decline should consult healthcare providers rather than relying solely on commercial brain training products, as professional guidance was associated with better outcomes in these studies.
