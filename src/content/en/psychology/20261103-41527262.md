## The key finding

A 2026 systematic review of 23 studies found that nurses with doctoral degrees—whether PhD or Doctor of Nursing Practice (DNP)—are not following the career paths their degrees were designed for. In the United States, PhD-trained nurses who were expected to pursue research are increasingly moving into clinical leadership positions, while DNP graduates originally trained for advanced clinical practice are entering academic faculty roles. Globally, PhD nurses occupy hybrid positions spanning research, teaching, and limited clinical work. Both groups report role misalignment, professional identity confusion, and insufficient institutional support for their evolving responsibilities.

## What the study looked like

Researchers conducted a scoping systematic review following PRISMA guidelines, searching five major databases (PROQUEST Nursing, Scopus, EBSCO, EMBASE, and PubMed) in October 2024. From an initial pool of 6,003 references, they identified 23 studies that met inclusion criteria examining career trajectories of doctorally-prepared nurses. The review focused specifically on how PhD and DNP nurses navigate the intersection of research and clinical practice in their actual working roles, both in the United States where the DNP degree exists as a distinct pathway, and internationally where PhD is typically the sole doctoral option in nursing. The research team used thematic analysis to synthesize findings across diverse geographic contexts and employment settings.

## Why researchers think this happened

The authors suggest that rigid distinctions between "research-focused PhD" and "practice-focused DNP" no longer reflect the complexity of modern healthcare systems. Healthcare organizations increasingly need leaders who can bridge the research-practice gap, applying evidence directly at the bedside while generating practice-based knowledge. PhD nurses may gravitate toward clinical leadership because these roles offer influence over patient care systems, while DNP graduates enter academia to teach the next generation of advanced practice nurses or because academic positions provide intellectual challenge beyond routine clinical work. Globally, the scarcity of doctorally-prepared nurses means institutions ask them to wear multiple hats regardless of degree type. The review connects these patterns to broader discussions in nursing literature about whether traditional doctoral pathways adequately prepare nurses for 21st-century healthcare demands.

## How to read this carefully

This review analyzed only 23 studies from thousands of initial references, suggesting limited published research on this topic. The findings describe associations and patterns rather than proving causation—we cannot say definitively why individual nurses choose particular career paths. Geographic bias exists since many studies focused on the United States, where the PhD-DNP distinction is most pronounced; findings may not generalize to countries with different nursing education systems. The review relied on published studies rather than directly surveying nurses, potentially missing unpublished experiences. "Role misalignment" and "identity challenges" are subjective experiences that vary by individual. The review does not evaluate whether nontraditional career paths lead to better patient outcomes or career satisfaction, only that they differ from original degree intentions.

## What this means for everyday life

If you are considering doctoral education in nursing, this review suggests you should not assume your degree type will determine your career ceiling. The boundaries are more fluid than catalog descriptions imply. For patients, this blurring might be positive: your hospital's clinical protocols might be shaped by a PhD nurse who understands both research methods and frontline realities, or you might learn from a DNP professor who brings cutting-edge practice knowledge into the classroom. For healthcare organizations, the findings suggest that rigid job descriptions requiring "PhD only" for research roles or "DNP only" for practice leadership may exclude qualified candidates. The review implies that nursing might benefit from career models that explicitly support hybrid roles—positions designed from the start to combine research, teaching, and clinical practice rather than forcing nurses into predefined boxes that no longer fit the work they actually do.
