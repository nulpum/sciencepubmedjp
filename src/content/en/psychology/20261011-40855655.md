## The key finding

Children and adolescents with cancer express a wide range of preferences for how they participate in treatment decision-making, according to a 2025 meta-synthesis of 39 qualitative studies involving young cancer patients under 18. Researchers extracted 156 distinct findings about treatment communication preferences and distilled them into three core themes: how children participate, when they choose not to participate, and why they engage. The analysis reveals that young patients' desired roles span a continuum—from passive listening to active co-decision-making—rather than falling into a single category. Most importantly, children rarely reported wanting complete control over treatment choices, but consistently valued being included in the conversation in age-appropriate ways.

## What the study looked like

This was a qualitative meta-synthesis—a method that systematically combines findings from multiple qualitative research studies to identify overarching patterns. Researchers searched medical databases with no date restrictions, ultimately reviewing 3,213 articles and including 55 that met strict criteria: studies had to include participants under 18 years old with childhood cancer, use qualitative or mixed methods, be written in English, and report findings directly from the children themselves rather than parent or clinician perspectives. From these 55 articles, 39 studies contributed specific findings about treatment decision-making preferences. The research team used meta-aggregation methods, a technique designed to preserve the authenticity of participant voices while identifying common themes across diverse study contexts. The included studies represented varied cancer diagnoses, treatment stages, and cultural settings.

## Why researchers think this happened

The meta-synthesis authors propose that children's varied preferences reflect developmental stages, personality differences, disease severity, and family dynamics. Some children may prefer passive roles when feeling overwhelmed by information or when trusting adults to protect their best interests. Others seek active participation to maintain a sense of control during an experience that strips away many childhood freedoms. The "why" findings suggest children participate to feel respected, to understand what's happening to their bodies, and to ensure their quality-of-life concerns are heard—factors that matter differently to each child. The research builds on prior work showing that patient engagement improves treatment adherence, but extends it by demonstrating that "engagement" itself is not one-size-fits-all. Children's preferences may also shift over time as they mature, gain experience with the healthcare system, or face changing prognoses.

## How to read this carefully

This synthesis captures self-reported preferences rather than measuring health outcomes tied to different communication approaches. The studies included were qualitative, meaning findings describe experiences and perspectives but cannot establish whether honoring these preferences improves survival rates, treatment completion, or psychological well-being. Selection bias is possible—children who participated in research interviews may differ from those who declined. Most included studies were conducted in high-income countries with established pediatric oncology programs, limiting generalizability to under-resourced settings. The synthesis also cannot account for how preferences might change when children face imminent end-of-life decisions versus curative treatment plans. Cultural factors around childhood autonomy and family decision-making structures vary globally, yet the review was limited to English-language publications.

## What this means for everyday life

For parents facing a child's cancer diagnosis, this research suggests there's no script for the "right" level of involvement—the right level is the one your specific child wants, which may surprise you and may change over time. Healthcare teams should consider routinely asking young patients how much they want to know and how they prefer decisions to be made, rather than assuming based on age alone. For older children and teens, being excluded entirely from conversations about their own bodies can feel disempowering, even if they don't want final say. Given this evidence, it might be worth considering regular check-ins about communication preferences as a standard part of pediatric cancer care, acknowledging that a seven-year-old's needs differ vastly from a seventeen-year-old's, and both deserve to have their voices heard in ways that feel comfortable to them.
