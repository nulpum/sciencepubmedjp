## The key finding

A comprehensive 2024 analysis of 25 studies involving 14,457 adults found that hedonic hunger — the drive to eat for pleasure rather than physical need — shows a positive association with body mass index (BMI). The correlation coefficient was 0.13, indicating a small but statistically significant relationship. This means that people who score higher on measures of food reward-seeking tend to have modestly higher BMIs, though the effect is considerably smaller than many researchers initially hypothesized. All three aspects of hedonic hunger measured by researchers — anticipating available food, being around present food, and tasting appealing food — showed similar positive associations with body weight.

## What the study looked like

Researchers conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis, searching three major scientific databases through January 2023 for studies examining hedonic hunger in healthy adults. They included only studies that used the Power of Food Scale (PFS), a validated questionnaire measuring the psychological drive to consume palatable foods across three situations: when food is available in the environment, when food is physically present, and when food is being tasted. Of the 25 included studies, 24 were cross-sectional (measuring both variables at one point in time) and one was a cohort study (following participants over time). Twenty-two studies included both men and women, while three focused specifically on gender-separated analyses. The research team used statistical methods to combine results across all studies, accounting for differences in study design and population characteristics.

## Why researchers think this happened

The positive association between hedonic hunger and BMI aligns with theories suggesting that modern food environments, rich in highly palatable and energy-dense foods, may trigger reward-seeking eating behaviors that contribute to weight gain over time. The researchers note that hedonic hunger represents responsiveness to food cues and the rewarding properties of eating, which may lead some individuals to consume more calories than their bodies require for energy balance. However, the weakness of the association suggests that hedonic hunger is just one factor among many influencing body weight. Other variables — including metabolic rate, physical activity levels, genetic predisposition, sleep patterns, and overall dietary patterns — likely play substantial roles. The small effect size indicates that hedonic hunger alone cannot explain much of the variation in BMI across individuals.

## How to read this carefully

Several important limitations affect how confidently we can interpret these findings. First, 24 of the 25 studies were cross-sectional, meaning they captured a snapshot in time rather than following people over months or years. This makes it impossible to determine whether hedonic hunger leads to higher BMI, whether higher BMI influences food cravings, or whether some third factor influences both. The researchers themselves noted the low quality of included studies. Second, all studies relied on self-reported questionnaires about food cravings, which may not fully capture actual eating behavior. Third, the small correlation (0.13) means hedonic hunger explains only about 1-2% of the variation in BMI between individuals. Meta-regression found no moderating effects of age, sex distribution, or baseline BMI, but unmeasured confounding factors could still influence the relationship.

## What this means for everyday life

This research suggests that the drive to eat for pleasure rather than hunger is linked to body weight, but the relationship is more nuanced than simple cause-and-effect. Given this modest association, it might be worth considering how your own eating patterns relate to genuine hunger versus the appeal of tasty foods — not as a source of guilt, but as useful self-knowledge. If you notice frequent eating in response to food cues (advertisements, cooking shows, walking past bakeries) rather than physical hunger, this awareness alone can be valuable. The findings also suggest that addressing weight concerns likely requires attention to multiple factors beyond just controlling food cravings. Environmental strategies like reducing exposure to highly palatable food cues, alongside attention to sleep, stress, and activity patterns, may be more effective than focusing solely on willpower around appealing foods.
