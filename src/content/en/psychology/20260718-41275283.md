## The key finding

A 2025 systematic review examining 28 studies with nearly 20,000 children found that physically enriching school playgrounds — adding features like painted line markings, climbing walls, and play equipment — was associated with modest increases in vigorous physical activity (VPA) and moderate-to-vigorous physical activity (MVPA). Specifically, enriched playgrounds showed effect sizes of 0.17 for both VPA and MVPA, and a notably larger effect of 0.81 for steps per minute in the studies that measured it. Line markings alone or combined with structures like climbing walls appeared most consistently linked to activity increases, while interventions that involved students in the design process paradoxically showed decreased MVPA.

## What the study looked like

Researchers conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis following established guidelines, searching seven major academic databases for studies published through 2025. They included 28 studies involving 19,753 participants — children and adolescents in preschool through high school settings. The studies examined various playground enrichment strategies: painted games and sports court markings on asphalt, installation of physical structures like climbing equipment, provision of loose equipment such as balls and jump ropes, and landscaping changes. Most studies used observational methods or accelerometers to measure physical activity during recess or break times, comparing enriched playgrounds to control conditions (typically unchanged playgrounds). Study quality varied considerably: only three studies had low risk of bias, while 11 had high risk and 14 raised some methodological concerns.

## Why researchers think this happened

The review authors drew on environmental enrichment theory to explain their findings. Line markings may increase activity by providing visual cues for games and sports, creating designated play zones that encourage organized active play. The combination of markings with physical structures potentially offers both guidance (where to play) and challenge (what to climb or navigate), appealing to different age groups and skill levels. The unexpected finding that involving end-users in design decreased MVPA might reflect children choosing aesthetically pleasing but less physically demanding features, or extended planning phases that delayed implementation. The short-term nature of MVPA increases suggests novelty effects — new playground features initially excite children, but activity levels may return toward baseline as the environment becomes familiar. The lack of effect on sedentary behavior or light-to-moderate activity suggests enrichment shifts the intensity of existing active play rather than converting inactive children into active ones.

## How to read this carefully

This review reveals significant limitations that prevent strong conclusions. The high heterogeneity across studies means results varied widely, and only three of 28 studies had robust methodology free from bias concerns. The MVPA benefits appeared significant only in the short term, raising questions about lasting impact. Studies measured different outcomes using different methods, making comparisons difficult. Most importantly, findings for cognitive development (attention, executive function), psychological wellbeing, and social outcomes like peer interactions were mixed and inconclusive due to few high-quality studies examining these dimensions. The research largely reflects correlation — enriched playgrounds were linked to more activity, but we cannot definitively say the enrichment caused the change, as many confounding factors (weather, teacher supervision, school culture) were difficult to control across studies.

## What this means for everyday life

For parents and educators considering playground improvements, this research suggests that relatively simple additions like painted games and activity markings might be worth exploring as one component of encouraging children's movement at school. Combined with climbing structures or other physical features, these environmental cues may help some children be more vigorously active during breaks. However, given the short-term nature of benefits and high study heterogeneity, it's important not to view playground redesign as a comprehensive solution to childhood inactivity. The inconclusive evidence on cognitive and social benefits means we shouldn't assume enriched playgrounds automatically improve focus, wellbeing, or peer relationships — though they might not hurt. The findings remind us that children's activity patterns respond to environmental invitations, but maintaining engagement likely requires ongoing novelty, program support, and attention to what actually appeals to the specific children using the space rather than adult assumptions about what should work.
