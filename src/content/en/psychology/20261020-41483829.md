## The key finding

A 2026 systematic review and meta-analysis examining 37 studies found that isoflavone supplementation is associated with modest improvements in cognitive function, with the most pronounced benefit seen in memory. Among 16 randomized controlled trials included in the meta-analysis, the pooled effect size for overall cognitive function was 0.25, while memory showed a stronger effect size of 0.33. These plant-derived compounds—found abundantly in soy and other legumes—have estrogen-like properties that researchers believe may influence brain function, particularly in aging populations.

## What the study looked like

Researchers conducted a comprehensive literature search across three major scientific databases (PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science) to identify relevant studies on isoflavone interventions and cognitive outcomes. They identified 37 total studies that met their criteria, with 16 randomized controlled trials containing sufficient data for meta-analysis. The research team assessed each study's methodological quality and risk of bias, finding that 27 studies scored positively while 9 received neutral ratings—none were rated as having high risk of bias. The analysis examined both composite cognitive function (overall mental performance) and specific cognitive domains including memory, executive function, psychomotor speed, and processing speed. Participants across studies were primarily aging adults, as isoflavones have been investigated mainly for age-related cognitive concerns.

## Why researchers think this happened

Isoflavones possess estrogen-like properties that may interact with estrogen receptors in the brain, potentially influencing neural pathways involved in memory formation and cognitive processing. The authors suggest that these compounds' neuroprotective effects could be particularly relevant for aging populations, where declining estrogen levels (especially in postmenopausal women) have been linked to cognitive changes. The stronger effect on memory compared to other cognitive domains may reflect isoflavones' specific influence on brain regions like the hippocampus, which is rich in estrogen receptors and critical for memory consolidation. The systematic review revealed mixed results across individual studies—15 showed positive outcomes, 7 showed bidirectional effects, 13 showed no significance, and only 2 showed negative effects—suggesting that individual responses may vary based on factors like baseline hormone levels, age, sex, or genetic differences in isoflavone metabolism.

## How to read this carefully

While these findings are noteworthy, several limitations warrant caution. The overall effect size of 0.25 for composite cognitive function is considered modest in statistical terms, meaning the real-world impact may be subtle rather than dramatic. The memory effect size of 0.33, though stronger, still represents a small-to-moderate benefit. The analysis found no significant effects on executive function, psychomotor speed, or processing speed, suggesting isoflavones don't universally enhance all aspects of cognition. Additionally, the variability across individual studies—with some showing no effect and others showing benefits—indicates that results may not apply equally to all people. Publication bias (where positive studies are more likely to be published) could also influence these pooled results. Most importantly, this research examined associations in controlled trials, not long-term disease prevention.

## What this means for everyday life

Given these findings, individuals interested in cognitive health—particularly those concerned about age-related memory changes—might consider incorporating soy-based foods like tofu, edamame, tempeh, or soy milk into their diets as part of a broader approach to brain health. The modest memory benefit associated with isoflavones in these studies suggests they could be one element of a multifaceted strategy that includes other evidence-based practices like regular exercise, quality sleep, and social engagement. However, it's worth noting that dietary sources of isoflavones vary in concentration, and the optimal dose remains unclear. Rather than viewing soy as a cognitive cure-all, consider it as a potentially beneficial addition to an already balanced diet, particularly if you're in an aging demographic where these compounds have been most studied.
