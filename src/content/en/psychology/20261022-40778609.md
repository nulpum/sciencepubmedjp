## The key finding

A 2026 network meta-analysis of 25 randomized controlled trials reveals that not all exercise combinations are equal for protecting cognitive function in people with cognitive impairment. Aerobic exercise combined with balance and flexibility training had a 70.8% probability of being the optimal combination for global cognition, showing a standardized mean difference of 1.07—a moderate-to-large effect. For executive function specifically (skills like planning, decision-making, and attention), aerobic exercise paired with resistance training ranked highest, with a 43.1% probability of being most effective. Overall, multicomponent exercise interventions improved global cognition with an effect size of 0.59 and executive function by 0.28, but did not significantly affect memory.

## What the study looked like

Researchers conducted a systematic review and network meta-analysis, searching six major databases through April 2025 for randomized controlled trials testing multicomponent exercise in people with cognitive impairment. The 25 included studies encompassed 2,298 participants total. Multicomponent exercise was defined as combining two or more types of physical activity—such as aerobic exercise (walking, cycling), resistance exercise (strength training), balance training, and flexibility work. The analysis compared different combinations head-to-head using statistical methods that rank interventions even when they haven't been directly compared in the same trial. Primary outcomes measured global cognition using standardized neuropsychological tests, with secondary outcomes tracking executive function and memory separately. The trials varied in duration and intensity, but all involved structured exercise programs rather than general physical activity.

## Why researchers think this happened

The authors suggest that different exercise types stimulate distinct neurobiological pathways. Aerobic exercise increases cerebral blood flow and promotes neuroplasticity—the brain's ability to form new neural connections—particularly in the hippocampus and prefrontal cortex. Balance and flexibility training may enhance these effects by engaging motor control systems and challenging spatial processing, which require integration across multiple brain regions. This multisystem engagement could explain why aerobic plus balance training showed the strongest effect on global cognition. For executive function, the combination of aerobic and resistance exercise may be particularly beneficial because resistance training activates growth factors like brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), which supports neuron survival and cognitive processing speed. The lack of significant memory effects might reflect that memory consolidation requires more specific cognitive training or longer intervention periods than these studies provided.

## How to read this carefully

This meta-analysis has important limitations. First, it synthesizes studies with varying intervention durations, exercise intensities, and participant characteristics—some had mild cognitive impairment, others had dementia. The "optimal" combinations showed statistical probability rankings, not guaranteed superiority for every individual. Second, network meta-analysis makes indirect comparisons between interventions that were never tested head-to-head in the same trial, introducing uncertainty. Third, the effect on memory was not significant, suggesting multicomponent exercise may not benefit all cognitive domains equally. Finally, the studies included were published research, which may overrepresent positive findings. Most participants were older adults, so these results may not generalize to younger people with cognitive impairment from other causes.

## What this means for everyday life

If you or a loved one is experiencing cognitive decline or has been diagnosed with mild cognitive impairment, this research suggests that the *type* of exercise matters as much as whether you exercise at all. Rather than focusing solely on walking or gym workouts, consider programs that blend aerobic activity with balance work—think tai chi combined with brisk walking, or dance classes that emphasize coordination. For those specifically concerned about executive function (managing daily tasks, staying organized), adding resistance training to aerobic exercise might be worth prioritizing. While this study cannot promise that exercise will reverse cognitive impairment, it offers evidence-based guidance on which combinations are linked to the strongest cognitive benefits. Consult with healthcare providers to tailor an exercise program to individual needs and abilities, especially if balance or mobility is already compromised.
