## What we're looking at

Chronic pain—pain persisting beyond three months—affects hundreds of millions of people worldwide, often resisting conventional medical treatment. When structural damage or disease doesn't fully explain the pain, clinicians increasingly turn to psychological interventions. These therapies don't treat pain as "all in your head." Instead, they help patients change how they respond to pain, potentially reducing both suffering and disability even when the sensation itself persists.

This review synthesizes evidence from eight systematic reviews and meta-analyses examining whether psychological interventions—cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT), acceptance and commitment therapy (ACT), mindfulness, behavioral therapy, and others—actually work for chronic pain. We focus on randomized controlled trials measuring outcomes like pain intensity, physical function, emotional distress, and quality of life. The evidence spans diverse chronic pain conditions: low back pain, neuropathic pain, needle-related procedural pain in children, and medically unexplained symptoms.

## The evidence — what studies actually found

**Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT)** emerged as one of the most-studied approaches. A 2022 network meta-analysis of chronic low back pain found that CBT combined with physiotherapy produced clinically important improvements in physical function (standardized mean difference 1.01) and pain intensity (0.92) immediately post-treatment [PMID 35354560]. However, when delivered alone without structured exercise, CBT showed more modest effects. Another comprehensive 2020 review of medically unexplained symptoms found high-intensity CBT reduced pain (SMD 0.54), anxiety (0.52), and depression (0.80) at treatment end, though effects diminished at long-term follow-up [PMID 32975190]. For individualized exercise programs, a 2022 meta-analysis demonstrated that combining exercise with psychological treatments—particularly behavioral-cognitive therapies—yielded a clinically meaningful 38% relative benefit over standard active treatments for chronic low back pain [PMID 35914641].

**Acceptance and commitment therapy (ACT)**, which focuses on increasing valued activities despite pain rather than eliminating pain itself, showed promise in a 2017 systematic review. ACT produced significant medium-to-large effects on pain acceptance and psychological flexibility, small-to-medium effects on functioning, anxiety, and depression, but notably did not significantly reduce pain intensity or improve quality of life compared to controls [PMID 27479642]. A more recent 2024 review specifically examined cognitive fusion—the tendency to become entangled with pain-related thoughts—and found psychological interventions decreased this process with medium effect sizes at long-term follow-up (g = -0.55), suggesting a potential mechanism through which these therapies work [PMID 39213942].

**Mindfulness-based stress reduction (MBSR)** was evaluated in a 2004 meta-analysis covering diverse clinical populations. Twenty studies meeting quality criteria showed overall effect sizes of approximately 0.5 for both physical and mental well-being measures, with both controlled and uncontrolled studies yielding similar magnitudes [PMID 15256293]. This suggests mindfulness helps people cope with clinical and nonclinical problems, though the relatively small number of studies at that time warranted caution.

**Behavioral therapy and pain education** showed particularly sustainable effects. The 2022 low back pain network meta-analysis found behavioral therapy maintained clinically important pain reduction until mid-term follow-up (six months; SMD 1.01, high-quality evidence), while pain education programs showed effects lasting at least to mid-term for physical function [PMID 35354560]. These interventions appeared most effective when combined with physiotherapy care, particularly structured exercise.

**Rehabilitation interventions for neuropathic pain**—often considered especially difficult to treat—were examined in a 2024 meta-analysis of 15 trials. Psychological interventions reduced both pain intensity (SMD -0.49) and pain-related disability (SMD -0.51), whereas other interventions affected pain but not disability, suggesting psychological approaches offer broader functional benefits [PMID 39101676].

**In children and adolescents** experiencing needle-related procedural pain, a 2018 Cochrane review found very low- to low-quality evidence supporting distraction for self-reported pain (SMD -0.56) and distress (SMD -0.82), and very low-quality evidence showing hypnosis reduced self-reported pain (SMD -1.40) and distress (SMD -2.53). Combined CBT showed efficacy for observer-reported pain and behavioral distress but not self-reported outcomes [PMID 30284240].

## How it might work — biological mechanisms

Psychological interventions don't simply distract from pain; they appear to modify how the brain processes pain signals and how individuals respond behaviorally to those signals. Several mechanisms likely contribute:

**Cognitive restructuring** helps patients identify and challenge unhelpful thoughts about pain—for example, catastrophizing ("this pain is unbearable and will never end") or fear-avoidance beliefs ("movement will damage me further"). By changing these thought patterns, CBT may reduce the emotional amplification of pain signals. The brain's pain experience involves both sensory-discriminative components (location, intensity) and affective-motivational components (unpleasantness, suffering). Psychological interventions primarily target the latter.

**Psychological flexibility**, the core target of ACT, involves accepting pain as present while still pursuing valued activities. Rather than struggling to eliminate pain—which often increases distress and disability—ACT teaches "willingness" to experience pain while doing what matters. The 2024 review found interventions reduced cognitive fusion, meaning patients became less dominated by pain-related thoughts [PMID 39213942]. This defusion allows mental space for other experiences and activities.

**Behavioral activation** addresses the withdrawal and inactivity that often accompany chronic pain. Pain naturally triggers protective responses like rest and guarding. While adaptive for acute injury, these behaviors can maintain disability and worsen pain when sustained long-term. Graded activity and behavioral therapy systematically increase physical and social engagement, potentially reversing deconditioning, improving mood through activity-based reward, and providing corrective learning experiences that movement isn't dangerous.

**Attention regulation** explains why distraction and mindfulness work. Pain demands attention; psychological interventions train alternative attention deployment. Distraction shifts focus away from pain signals. Mindfulness cultivates a particular quality of attention—present-focused, non-judgmental observation—that may reduce the struggle against pain and the secondary suffering that struggle creates. The 2004 MBSR meta-analysis suggested enhanced moment-to-moment awareness provides more accurate perception and reduces negative affect [PMID 15256293].

**Neuroplastic changes** may underlie sustained effects. While most reviews didn't directly measure brain changes, research outside these meta-analyses has shown psychological interventions can alter pain-related brain activity in regions like the anterior cingulate cortex and prefrontal cortex. These changes suggest learning and neuroplasticity, not just temporary coping.

## Where the evidence is strong (and weak)

The strongest evidence supports **CBT combined with exercise/physiotherapy for chronic low back pain**. The 2022 network meta-analysis provided moderate-quality evidence for immediate post-treatment benefits, with some effects sustained to mid-term follow-up [PMID 35354560]. High-intensity CBT showed consistent benefits across pain intensity, physical function, and psychological outcomes.

**Behavioral therapy and pain education** similarly showed robust, sustainable effects for low back pain when combined with physiotherapy. The 2022 review noted high-quality evidence for behavioral therapy's pain reduction through mid-term follow-up [PMID 35354560]. This represents some of the most reliable evidence in the psychological pain literature.

**Distraction and hypnosis for children's needle pain** demonstrated efficacy with clinically meaningful effect sizes, though evidence quality was rated very low to low due to methodological limitations common in psychological trials [PMID 30284240].

Evidence is **weaker or mixed** for:

**Long-term effects** (beyond 12 months): Most reviews found effects diminished at long-term follow-up. The 2020 review of medically unexplained symptoms noted "few effects remained at long-term follow-up" [PMID 32975190]. The 2022 individualized exercise review questioned sustainability beyond 12 months [PMID 35914641]. This suggests many patients may need ongoing support or booster sessions.

**Somatization or overall symptom load**: While interventions helped specific symptoms like pain or fatigue, complex measures of overall somatic symptom burden showed fewer benefits [PMID 32975190]. This may indicate psychological interventions work better for single dominant symptoms than diffuse, multiple unexplained complaints.

**Quality of life**: ACT notably failed to improve quality of life measures despite benefits for functioning and psychological outcomes [PMID 27479642]. Other reviews inconsistently reported quality-of-life gains, suggesting this outcome may be less responsive to psychological intervention than pain intensity or disability.

**Standalone psychological interventions**: Several reviews emphasized that psychological treatments worked best when combined with active treatments like exercise. The 2022 low back pain review explicitly stated psychological interventions are "most effective when delivered in conjunction with physiotherapy care" [PMID 35354560]. This challenges the notion that psychological therapy alone suffices for chronic pain.

**General practitioner-delivered interventions**: The 2020 review found GP-led behavioral interventions showed no significant beneficial effects, despite the primary care setting being perceived as appropriate [PMID 32975190]. This suggests the therapeutic relationship and setting matter, but therapist expertise and training may be crucial.

## Practical takeaways

For patients with chronic pain:

**Seek combination approaches**: Evidence strongly suggests psychological interventions work best alongside active physical treatments—particularly structured, individualized exercise programs. The relative benefit of combining psychological therapy with exercise is approximately 38% greater than exercise alone for chronic low back pain [PMID 35914641].

**Consider intervention type based on your goals**: If your primary concern is reducing pain intensity and improving function, high-intensity CBT combined with exercise shows the strongest evidence. If you struggle more with pain-related distress, catastrophizing, or feeling controlled by pain, ACT's focus on psychological flexibility and values-based action may be particularly relevant [PMID 27479642].

**Expect gradual improvement, not immediate cure**: Clinically meaningful effects typically emerge by treatment end but often represent moderate rather than complete relief. Effect sizes in the 0.5-0.8 range translate to noticeable but not transformative changes for many individuals.

**Plan for maintenance**: Since long-term effects frequently diminish, discuss with providers how to sustain benefits—whether through periodic booster sessions, ongoing self-practice, or transitioning to self-guided approaches after initial therapist-led treatment.

For healthcare providers:

**Individualize exercise within multidisciplinary programs**: The 2022 meta-analysis emphasized that individualizing exercise—tailoring type, intensity, and progression to the patient—adds clinical value with relatively little extra effort [PMID 35914641]. Motor-control-based treatments combined with behavioral therapy showed particular promise.

**Don't expect psychological intervention to replace biomedical assessment**: These interventions help patients manage chronic pain more effectively; they don't diagnose or treat underlying pathology. Adequate examination to rule out serious conditions remains essential.

**Mindfulness and ACT offer alternatives when CBT hasn't helped**: Different psychological approaches target different mechanisms. For patients who haven't responded to traditional CBT, mindfulness-based approaches or ACT focusing on acceptance and values may engage different processes. The evidence for mindfulness showed effect sizes around 0.5 across diverse populations [PMID 15256293].

**Neuropathic pain responds to psychological intervention**: Neuropathic pain—often considered primarily biomedical—showed meaningful responses to psychological interventions with effects on both pain intensity and disability [PMID 39101676]. This expands the potential application beyond musculoskeletal conditions.

## Limitations of this review

This synthesis reviewed systematic reviews and meta-analyses rather than individual trials, meaning we're interpreting others' interpretations of the primary data. Our conclusions are only as strong as the quality of those source reviews.

**Heterogeneity** was a pervasive problem. Interventions labeled "CBT" varied enormously in content, duration, therapist training, delivery format (individual vs. group), and theoretical emphasis. Pain populations ranged from specific diagnoses like neuropathic pain to vague categories like medically unexplained symptoms. This makes confident conclusions about "what works for whom" difficult.

**Quality of evidence** was predominantly low to very low across reviews. Common problems included lack of allocation concealment, inability to blind participants in behavioral trials, selective outcome reporting, and small sample sizes. The 2018 needle-pain review exemplified this, rating most evidence as very low quality despite apparent efficacy [PMID 30284240].

**Publication bias** likely inflates reported effects. Null findings are less likely to be published, and several reviews noted evidence suggesting publication bias. The true effect sizes may be smaller than these meta-analyses suggest.

**Sparse networks** in network meta-analyses meant many potential comparisons between intervention types lacked direct evidence, requiring indirect estimation with greater uncertainty [PMID 35354560].

**Mechanisms remain largely assumed**: While we described plausible mechanisms, most included studies didn't directly measure proposed mechanisms like cognitive fusion, attention regulation, or brain changes. The 2024 cognitive fusion review was a notable exception [PMID 39213942], but even there, establishing that fusion reduction causes pain improvement requires mediational analyses often not performed.

**Cost-effectiveness** evidence was limited and inconsistent. The 2020 review found ICERs ranging from £1,397 to £129,267, with considerable uncertainty [PMID 32975190]. We cannot confidently say whether psychological interventions for chronic pain represent good value for healthcare systems.

**Cultural and contextual factors** received little attention. Most studies came from Western healthcare contexts. Acceptability and effectiveness may differ across cultures with different conceptualizations of pain, mind-body relationships, and treatment preferences.

## Bottom line

Psychological interventions—particularly cognitive behavioral therapy, acceptance and commitment therapy, behavioral therapy, and mindfulness approaches—show genuine efficacy for chronic pain when outcomes are measured immediately post-treatment and at short-term follow-up (under six months). Effects are most robust when psychological therapy is combined with active physical treatments like individualized exercise programs. For chronic low back pain specifically, the combination of high-intensity CBT with physiotherapy shows moderate-quality evidence for meaningful improvements in both pain intensity and physical function.

Effect sizes typically fall in the small-to-medium range (0.4-0.8), representing clinically meaningful but not complete relief for most patients. These interventions appear to work by changing how people think about pain, respond behaviorally to pain signals, and deploy attention—not by eliminating the pain signal itself. Importantly, psychological interventions can reduce pain-related disability even when pain intensity changes modestly.

The evidence is weaker for long-term maintenance of benefits beyond one year, for improving overall quality of life, and for treating complex presentations with multiple unexplained symptoms. General practitioner-delivered interventions haven't shown the same benefits as specialist-delivered psychological therapy.

Given the relatively low risk, the evidence favors offering psychological interventions as part of multidisciplinary chronic pain management, with realistic expectations: moderate symptom improvement, enhanced function, and better coping rather than complete pain elimination. The challenge moving forward is understanding which patients benefit most from which psychological approaches, and how to sustain the initial benefits over the long term.

## Studies referenced

- [PMID 35354560](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35354560/) — Network meta-analysis of 97 RCTs (13,136 participants) examining psychological interventions for chronic low back pain; found CBT and pain education combined with physiotherapy produced clinically important improvements in function and pain.

- [PMID 15256293](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15256293/) — Meta-analysis of 20 studies on mindfulness-based stress reduction showing effect sizes of approximately 0.5 for physical and mental well-being across clinical and nonclinical populations with conditions including pain, cancer, and anxiety.

- [PMID 27479642](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27479642/) — Systematic review and meta-analysis of 11 trials testing acceptance and commitment therapy for chronic pain; found significant medium-to-large effects on pain acceptance and psychological flexibility but not on pain intensity or quality of life.

- [PMID 30284240](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30284240/) — Cochrane review of 59 trials (5,550 children) on psychological interventions for needle-related pain; found very low- to low-quality evidence supporting distraction, hypnosis, and combined CBT for reducing pain and distress.

- [PMID 39101676](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39101676/) — Systematic review and meta-analysis of 15 RCTs (764 participants) on rehabilitation interventions for neuropathic pain; found psychological interventions reduced both pain intensity (SMD -0.49) and pain-related disability (SMD -0.51).

- [PMID 35914641](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35914641/) — Meta-analysis of 58 studies (10,084 participants) on individualized exercise for chronic low back pain; found combining individualized exercise with psychological interventions produced clinically important effects with 38% relative benefit over active controls.

- [PMID 39213942](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39213942/) — Systematic review and meta-analysis of 18 articles (24 studies) examining whether psychological interventions decrease cognitive fusion in chronic pain patients; found small-to-medium effects at post-test (g = -0.39) and medium effects at long-term follow-up (g = -0.55).

- [PMID 32975190](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32975190/) — Comprehensive systematic review and economic evaluation of behavioral interventions for medically unexplained symptoms in primary care; found high-intensity CBT showed beneficial effects for pain, anxiety, and depression at treatment end, but few effects remained at long-term follow-up and cost-effectiveness varied widely.
