## What we're looking at

The concept of the "highly sensitive person" (HSP) has permeated popular psychology, self-help literature, and social media discussions about personality differences. The idea centers on sensory processing sensitivity (SPS)—a temperament trait characterized by heightened awareness of environmental stimuli, deeper cognitive processing, emotional reactivity, and overstimulation in intense environments. Proponents suggest that approximately 30% of the population scores high in SPS, experiencing the world with greater intensity than their less-sensitive peers [PMID 40446065].

But what does the scientific literature actually reveal about this trait? Is sensory sensitivity a distinct, measurable construct with clear biological underpinnings, or a loosely defined collection of overlapping personality features? This review synthesizes recent research examining how scientists measure sensory processing differences, what these measurements predict about mental health and behavior, and where significant gaps in our understanding remain.

## The evidence — what studies actually found

The research landscape around sensory processing sensitivity spans multiple fields, from autism research to vision science, with surprisingly little direct focus on the HSP construct itself. What we find instead are parallel investigations into sensory processing differences across various populations, each revealing pieces of a larger puzzle.

In autism research, sensory processing differences have received substantial empirical attention. A 2024 systematic review and meta-analysis examined 63 studies involving 11,659 autistic participants, finding robust associations between various types of sensory processing differences and mental health outcomes [PMID 39515075]. The research identified eleven distinct types of sensory processing differences, with hypersensitivity showing the strongest correlations with both internalizing problems (like anxiety and depression) and externalizing problems (like aggression and rule-breaking behavior). Visual, auditory, and tactile sensitivities demonstrated particularly strong associations, while unusual processing of smell and taste showed smaller effects.

Interestingly, the pattern wasn't uniform across all sensory subtypes. Sensation seeking—a tendency to pursue intense sensory experiences—was highly linked with externalizing problems but showed the weakest association with internalizing problems. This suggests that different facets of sensory processing may operate through distinct pathways and produce different psychological outcomes.

A 2025 systematic review specifically focused on assessment tools for both central sensitivity syndrome (CSS) and sensory processing sensitivity identified 29 validated questionnaires and scales designed to measure these constructs [PMID 41649871]. The instruments collectively assessed 78 variables grouped into categories including psychological factors, physical/psychosomatic aspects, intolerances, environmental sensitivity, and specific sensory modalities. The review found that more recent validation studies demonstrated better methodological quality, with the Central Sensitization Inventory Short Form scoring highest on quality assessments.

Research on individual differences in responsiveness to environmental cues provides additional context. A 2025 meta-analysis examining trait suggestibility—responsiveness to verbal suggestions—and nocebo responding (experiencing adverse effects from inactive stimuli) found a weak but significant positive correlation (r = .21) across 13 studies [PMID 39707685]. More suggestible individuals displayed larger nocebo responses, and this effect strengthened in studies with better methodological rigor, including those maintaining experimenter blinding. This suggests that individual differences in environmental responsiveness may indeed predict how people react to contextual cues, though the effects are modest.

The differential susceptibility model—central to HSP theory—proposes that highly sensitive individuals experience both greater harm in negative environments and greater benefit in positive ones. A 2025 protocol for a systematic review specifically examining SPS in caregiving contexts notes that this model "predisposes individuals to feeling the harmful effects of negative environments and to flourish in positive ones" [PMID 40446065]. However, this protocol represents planned research rather than completed findings, highlighting that systematic investigation of SPS correlates in specific contexts is still emerging.

## How it might work — biological mechanisms

The biological mechanisms underlying sensory processing sensitivity remain incompletely understood, but research offers several clues about potential pathways. The concept intersects with broader neuroscience findings about sensory gating, emotional reactivity, and individual differences in neural processing.

Sensory processing differences likely involve variations in how the nervous system filters, amplifies, and integrates incoming stimuli. In autism research, sensory hypersensitivity has been linked to altered neural gain control—essentially, the brain's volume knob for sensory signals [PMID 39515075]. When this system operates differently, stimuli that most people filter as background noise may reach conscious awareness with greater intensity, potentially overwhelming cognitive and emotional resources.

The relationship between sensory sensitivity and mental health problems may operate through multiple mechanisms. Higher sensory sensitivity could increase exposure to aversive stimuli, creating more opportunities for stress and negative emotional experiences. Alternatively, sensory differences might reflect underlying differences in emotional reactivity or threat detection systems. The finding that sensation seeking (pursuing intense experiences) relates differently to mental health outcomes than hypersensitivity (avoiding intense experiences) suggests these aren't simply opposite ends of a single dimension [PMID 39515075].

Research on suggestibility provides insight into how cognitive factors might amplify or modulate sensory experiences. The meta-analysis on nocebo responding found that individuals more responsive to verbal suggestions experienced stronger adverse reactions to inactive stimuli [PMID 39707685]. This suggests that expectations, attention, and interpretive frameworks may shape how sensory information translates into conscious experience—a cognitive layer atop basic sensory processing.

The concept of differential susceptibility implies neurobiological plasticity—nervous systems that are more responsive to environmental influences in both positive and negative directions. While the HSP literature often frames this as advantageous sensitivity, the autism research reveals a more complex picture where sensory differences consistently predict mental health challenges across multiple domains [PMID 39515075].

## Where the evidence is strong (and weak)

The evidence base reveals notable strengths and considerable weaknesses. On the strong side, we have robust documentation that sensory processing differences exist, vary across individuals, and correlate with meaningful psychological outcomes. The autism meta-analysis provides particularly solid evidence, synthesizing findings across 63 studies with clear, consistent patterns [PMID 39515075]. Specific sensory modalities (visual, auditory, tactile) show reliable associations with mental health problems, with effect sizes that are clinically meaningful.

The methodological rigor of assessment tools has improved substantially over time, with recent validations demonstrating lower risk of bias and more adequate psychometric properties [PMID 41649871]. This suggests that researchers are developing increasingly sophisticated methods to measure sensory processing differences, even if consensus on the best approach remains elusive.

However, significant weaknesses pervade the literature. Most fundamentally, heterogeneity is a persistent problem. The autism meta-analysis noted substantial heterogeneity in effect size estimates, indicating that relationships between sensory processing and mental health vary considerably across studies [PMID 39515075]. This variability could reflect genuine population differences, methodological variations, or inconsistency in how constructs are defined and measured.

The sensory processing sensitivity construct itself suffers from definitional imprecision. The systematic review of assessment tools identified 78 different variables across 29 instruments, spanning psychological traits, physical symptoms, environmental sensitivities, and sensory modalities [PMID 41649871]. This proliferation suggests that "sensory processing sensitivity" may not be a unitary construct but rather an umbrella term covering multiple, potentially distinct phenomena.

Longitudinal evidence is notably scarce. The autism review explicitly noted "a lack of longitudinal studies" as a limitation [PMID 39515075], making it difficult to determine whether sensory differences cause mental health problems, result from them, or share common underlying causes. Without temporal data, claims about sensory sensitivity as a risk factor or protective factor remain speculative.

Furthermore, most research comes from specific populations—primarily autism research or clinical samples—with limited investigation of sensory processing sensitivity in general populations or specific contexts like caregiving. The protocol for examining SPS in caregivers acknowledges this gap [PMID 40446065], but completed research in this area is not yet available.

External validity concerns also emerge from study characteristics. Research populations skew heavily toward white participants in high-income settings, limiting generalizability. The autism meta-analysis included 11,659 participants but didn't report demographic diversity [PMID 39515075], while other reviewed studies similarly lacked diverse samples.

## Practical takeaways

What can individuals and practitioners take from this evidence base? Several points warrant consideration:

**Sensory processing differences are real and measurable.** Multiple validated instruments exist to assess various facets of sensory sensitivity, from hypersensitivity to specific modalities to broader patterns of environmental responsiveness [PMID 41649871]. People who report feeling overwhelmed by sensory stimuli or more emotionally reactive to environmental changes aren't imagining these experiences—they're describing patterns that researchers can reliably detect and quantify.

**Sensory sensitivity associates with mental health challenges.** Across multiple studies and populations, higher sensory sensitivity correlates with greater anxiety, depression, and behavioral problems [PMID 39515075]. This doesn't mean sensory sensitivity causes these problems, but individuals experiencing both should consider whether sensory overwhelm contributes to their distress.

**Different types of sensory sensitivity may require different approaches.** Hypersensitivity (being overwhelmed by stimuli) and sensation seeking (craving intense experiences) show distinct patterns of association with mental health outcomes [PMID 39515075]. Interventions focused on reducing sensory overload may help individuals with hypersensitivity, while those high in sensation seeking might benefit from finding safe outlets for their need for intense experiences.

**Context matters enormously.** The differential susceptibility model suggests that sensitive individuals may suffer more in negative environments but thrive in positive ones [PMID 40446065]. This implies that environmental modification—creating supportive, low-stress contexts—may be particularly beneficial for highly sensitive individuals, though direct evidence for this proposition remains limited.

**Individual differences in responsiveness extend beyond sensation.** The suggestibility research reveals that people vary in how strongly they respond to verbal cues and expectations [PMID 39707685]. Highly sensitive individuals might benefit from awareness that their responsiveness encompasses not just sensory stimuli but also cognitive and social information.

## Limitations of this review

This synthesis faces several constraints. Most significantly, the available literature doesn't focus specifically on "highly sensitive persons" as a population. Instead, we've synthesized research on sensory processing differences across various contexts—autism, clinical populations, and general individual differences. While these studies inform our understanding of sensory sensitivity, they may not directly validate the specific HSP construct as popularly conceived.

The included studies vary widely in methodological quality, measurement approaches, and populations studied. We've drawn connections across disparate research areas (autism, visual perception, suggestibility), which may obscure important distinctions between how sensory processing operates in different contexts and populations.

We excluded several studies from our synthesis due to minimal relevance to sensory processing sensitivity. Two studies focused on visual cycle modulators for age-related macular degeneration [PMID 33331670], vision impairment and driving safety [PMID 37567751], and deep learning algorithms for detecting myopic maculopathy [PMID 33610832, PMID 27123863]—topics peripheral to sensory processing sensitivity as a personality or temperament trait.

Publication bias likely affects this literature. Studies finding null relationships between sensory sensitivity and outcomes may be underrepresented, potentially inflating apparent effect sizes. The heterogeneity noted across meta-analyses suggests that published findings may not represent the full picture.

Finally, this review cannot address causality. The correlational nature of most included research means we cannot determine whether sensory sensitivity causes mental health problems, whether psychological distress increases sensory sensitivity, or whether both result from other factors.

## Bottom line

Sensory processing sensitivity represents a legitimate area of scientific inquiry with measurable individual differences that correlate with important psychological outcomes. The evidence strongly supports that people vary in how intensely they process sensory information and respond to environmental stimuli, and these differences associate with mental health, particularly anxiety and emotional reactivity.

However, the popular conception of the "highly sensitive person" as a distinct, well-defined category affecting 30% of the population outpaces the scientific evidence. The research reveals a complex, multifaceted picture of sensory processing differences that vary by modality, context, and population. Different types of sensitivity (hypersensitivity versus sensation seeking) predict different outcomes, and substantial heterogeneity across studies suggests we're still refining our understanding of what we're measuring.

For individuals who identify as highly sensitive, the research validates that sensory and emotional reactivity differences are real and consequential. The evidence suggests that managing sensory environments, recognizing individual needs, and seeking supportive contexts may be beneficial, though direct intervention research remains limited. For researchers, the field needs more longitudinal studies, diverse populations, clearer construct definitions, and investigation of sensory processing sensitivity in specific contexts beyond autism and clinical samples.

The science of sensory processing sensitivity is evolving, with increasingly rigorous measurement tools and growing attention to mechanisms. But we're still in relatively early stages of understanding how sensory sensitivity operates, what predicts it, and how best to support individuals who experience the world with heightened intensity.
