## The key finding

The Korean Society of Thyroid Radiology (KSThR) released new standardized ultrasound imaging protocols in 2025 for monitoring patients with papillary thyroid microcarcinoma (PTMC)—thyroid cancers smaller than 1 centimeter—who choose active surveillance instead of immediate surgery. This guideline aims to make active surveillance safer and more consistent by establishing clear imaging methods for detecting tumor growth, spread beyond the thyroid capsule, or lymph node metastasis. The review compared Korea's approach with major international guidelines, finding both shared principles and differences in how frequently patients should be scanned and what tumor changes warrant switching to surgery.

## What the study looked like

This 2025 publication is a review article, not a clinical trial with patients. The authors examined the KSThR guideline alongside existing international consensus statements on active surveillance for low-risk thyroid cancer. They analyzed recommendations for ultrasound technique, measurement protocols, follow-up intervals, and criteria for identifying tumor progression. The focus was on papillary thyroid microcarcinoma, which accounts for the majority of thyroid cancers detected in recent decades and typically grows very slowly. The review synthesized guidance on patient selection—who qualifies for monitoring rather than surgery—and the specific ultrasound features radiologists should document at baseline and during follow-up visits.

## Why researchers think this happened

The push for standardized imaging protocols reflects two decades of evidence that many PTMCs behave indolently, with studies from Japan and Korea showing that 5-10% of surveilled tumors grow significantly, while the majority remain stable or even shrink. Researchers recognized that without consistent imaging methods, radiologists might measure tumors differently or interpret subtle changes inconsistently, leading some patients to unnecessary surgery while others might miss true progression. The KSThR guideline emerged because active surveillance has become more common in East Asia but varied widely in practice. International guidelines share the core principle that very small, low-risk thyroid cancers often don't require immediate treatment, but differ on details like how much growth (3 mm versus 5 mm increase) should trigger intervention, reflecting different tolerance for uncertainty across medical cultures.

## How to read this carefully

This is a guideline review, not a study proving active surveillance works better than surgery for any individual patient. The recommendations assume careful patient selection—excluding those with tumors near critical structures, aggressive ultrasound features, or lymph node involvement at diagnosis. Real-world outcomes depend heavily on patient adherence to follow-up schedules and radiologist skill in detecting subtle changes. The review doesn't address psychological factors: some patients experience significant anxiety monitoring a known cancer, which might outweigh the physical risks of surgery. Also, while the guidelines compared are recent, most underlying evidence comes from Asian populations; applicability to other ethnic groups with different thyroid cancer biology remains less studied.

## What this means for everyday life

If you or someone you know is diagnosed with a very small thyroid nodule confirmed as cancer, this guideline suggests it's worth asking whether active surveillance is an option rather than assuming surgery is the only path. The existence of standardized imaging protocols means doctors can now monitor these cancers more reliably, potentially sparing patients surgical risks like vocal cord nerve damage or lifelong thyroid hormone replacement. However, active surveillance requires commitment to regular ultrasounds (often every 6-12 months initially) and comfort with uncertainty. Given this review, patients considering surveillance might ask their endocrinologist whether their imaging center follows established protocols, since consistency in measurement technique directly affects whether true progression gets detected early enough to intervene safely.
