Margaret Dayhoff's classic hypothesis proposed that complex proteins arose when simpler peptides duplicated and then fused together, expanding life's molecular toolkit. While researchers have extensively studied gene duplication over the past 20 years, the fusion step has received far less attention. This perspective argues that fusion is just as critical as mutation or insertion in protein evolution. When duplicated protein segments (protomers) fuse, they can either preserve their original ancestral function or evolve completely new capabilities. By merging separate domains into single chains, fusion dramatically expands the structural and functional space that proteins can explore—essentially giving evolution more raw material to work with when crafting molecular machines.
