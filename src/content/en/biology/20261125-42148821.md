## The key finding

Researchers have bridged two traditionally separate fields—molecular biology and evolutionary biology—by showing how hormonal pleiotropy (when one hormone affects multiple traits at once) creates patterns of trait integration that persist across generations. Working primarily with steroid hormones in Anolis and Sceloporus lizards, scientists demonstrated that hormone-driven trait coordination operates at scales from gene expression to whole-organism morphology, and that the same hormonal pathways structuring phenotypes today also determine which evolutionary trajectories are possible tomorrow. Importantly, the study reveals that responsiveness to hormones like testosterone can itself evolve, meaning closely related species can develop entirely different patterns of trait integration through divergent hormonal regulation.

## What the study looked like

This 2026 paper is a conceptual synthesis drawing heavily on the authors' experimental work with two lizard genera over multiple years. Rather than presenting a single controlled experiment, the researchers integrated data from quantitative genetic breeding designs (which track trait inheritance across generations), hormone manipulation experiments (administering or blocking steroid hormones to measure effects on multiple traits), and transcriptomic analyses (examining how thousands of genes respond to hormonal signals). The lizard studies measured diverse traits including body size, coloration, behavior, and gene expression profiles. The work combined observational field data with laboratory hormone dosing experiments, then linked these mechanistic findings to quantitative genetic theory that predicts how trait covariances evolve under selection.

## Why researchers think this happened

The authors propose that hormonal pleiotropy creates a mechanistic foundation for phenotypic integration because hormone receptors are distributed across tissues throughout the body. When testosterone (or another steroid) circulates, it simultaneously affects muscle, bone, brain, and skin cells—wherever receptors exist. This generates correlations among traits: individuals with high testosterone levels develop larger muscles, brighter colors, and more aggressive behaviors together, not independently. Crucially, if genetic variation exists in how tissues respond to the same hormonal signal, then selection on one hormone-regulated trait (say, aggression) indirectly affects other traits regulated by that same hormone (like muscle mass). The discussion section emphasizes that prior evolutionary studies often treated trait correlations as statistical abstractions, while mechanistic studies focused narrowly on pathways without considering evolutionary consequences. Hormonal pleiotropy provides the missing link: a concrete molecular mechanism that generates the genetic covariances evolutionary biologists measure in breeding experiments.

## How to read this carefully

This is a theoretical synthesis and framework paper, not a single controlled experiment with replicable statistical tests. The lizard examples illustrate concepts but don't constitute comprehensive evidence across taxa—hormonal systems vary dramatically among vertebrates, and findings in lizards may not generalize to mammals, birds, or fish. The authors acknowledge that measuring genetic covariances requires large breeding designs that are logistically challenging for most species. Additionally, the paper focuses on steroid hormones; other hormone classes (peptides, amines) may create different integration patterns. The concept that hormonal pleiotropy constrains evolution is based on quantitative genetic models that make simplifying assumptions (constant environments, additive genetic variance). Real populations experience fluctuating selection and non-additive genetic effects that complicate predictions.

## What this means for everyday life

This research helps explain why traits often change together in predictable clusters rather than independently. If you've noticed that athletic training doesn't just build muscle but also affects mood, energy, and even skin appearance, hormonal pleiotropy provides a mechanistic explanation: exercise alters hormone levels (testosterone, cortisol, growth hormone) that coordinate changes across multiple body systems simultaneously. The finding that hormone responsiveness itself can evolve suggests that populations adapting to new environments might alter how their bodies respond to the same hormonal signals, rather than changing hormone levels alone. For anyone interested in fitness, health, or even animal breeding, this framework suggests that attempting to change one trait (muscle mass, aggression, coloration) through interventions affecting hormones will inevitably influence a suite of correlated traits—some desirable, some not—because those traits share common regulatory control.
