## The key finding

A 2026 review in the *Annual Review of Neuroscience* highlights that pregnancy triggers profound, simultaneous remodeling across multiple body systems—not just the reproductive organs. Recent longitudinal human studies reveal that the maternal brain undergoes significant structural changes that occur in sync with surges in reproductive hormones. These neural adaptations don't happen in isolation: they unfold alongside major shifts in immune regulation, vascular system expansion, and even changes to the gut microbiome. Understanding pregnancy's impact on the brain requires looking at the whole body as an interconnected system.

## What the study looked like

This is a comprehensive scientific review that synthesizes findings from recent longitudinal studies tracking pregnant individuals over time. The authors examined research on human pregnancies, focusing on studies that measured changes in the nervous system, endocrine (hormone) system, cardiovascular system, immune function, and gut microbiome from conception through the postpartum period. Unlike a single experiment, this review integrates data from multiple research teams who have used brain imaging, blood tests, immune cell profiling, and microbiome sequencing to map how pregnancy transforms the body. The review considers both typical pregnancies and those complicated by adverse health events, aiming to identify biological patterns that distinguish these experiences.

## Why researchers think this happened

The authors propose that pregnancy-related brain changes are part of an evolutionarily coordinated response designed to support both fetal development and maternal caregiving. As the placenta ramps up production of estrogen, progesterone, and other hormones, these signals don't just prepare the uterus—they also trigger widespread adaptations. The immune system shifts toward a more tolerant state to avoid rejecting the fetus, which shares only half the mother's genetic material. The cardiovascular system expands blood volume by roughly 50% to nourish the placenta. These peripheral changes send biochemical signals to the brain through circulating hormones, immune molecules called cytokines, and even metabolites produced by gut bacteria. The brain, in turn, reorganizes circuits involved in emotion regulation, stress response, and social behavior—changes that may help prepare for the demands of caring for a newborn. Prior research had often studied these systems separately; this review argues that their simultaneous coordination is key to understanding maternal neurobiology.

## How to read this carefully

This is a review paper, not a controlled experiment, meaning it synthesizes existing research rather than presenting new data. The longitudinal human studies cited are still relatively recent and involve limited sample sizes, so findings will need replication across diverse populations. Importantly, the observed brain changes are correlations—we know they occur alongside hormonal and immune shifts, but direct cause-and-effect relationships remain difficult to prove in humans. Most studies have focused on healthy pregnancies in well-resourced settings, so the findings may not fully capture experiences across different socioeconomic backgrounds, healthcare access levels, or pregnancy complications. The review also acknowledges that distinguishing between adaptive changes and those linked to adverse outcomes requires much more research.

## What this means for everyday life

This research reframes pregnancy as a whole-body transformation rather than an isolated reproductive event. For pregnant individuals, it suggests that the physical and emotional changes they experience—from shifts in immune function to changes in mood and perception—are interconnected biological adaptations, not separate phenomena. Given this systemic remodeling, it might be worth considering pregnancy-related mental health concerns (like postpartum mood changes) as potentially linked to immune, vascular, or hormonal shifts that could be addressed holistically. For healthcare providers, the findings underscore the value of monitoring multiple body systems during and after pregnancy. For researchers, this integrative perspective opens new avenues for understanding why some pregnancies lead to long-term health changes and how to better support maternal well-being across the entire perinatal period.
