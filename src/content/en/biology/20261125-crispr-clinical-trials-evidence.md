## What we're looking at

For decades, CRISPR gene editing lived in the lab—a theoretical tool promising to rewrite the genetic code and cure inherited diseases. That era is over. In late 2023, the FDA approved Casgevy (exagamglogene autotemcel), the first CRISPR-based drug for humans, targeting severe sickle cell disease and transfusion-dependent β-thalassemia [PMID 38786024]. By early 2025, more than 120 clinical trials are testing CRISPR-based therapies across blood disorders, cancer, cardiovascular disease, and even HIV [PMID 40160040]. This review synthesizes the latest evidence from completed and ongoing trials, examining what CRISPR can actually accomplish in patients, how it works at the molecular level, where the evidence is strongest, and what limitations remain before gene editing becomes routine medicine.

## The evidence — what studies actually found

### Blood disorders: the clinical success story

The most robust human data come from sickle cell disease (SCD) and β-thalassemia trials. Both conditions arise from mutations in hemoglobin genes—sickle cell from a single nucleotide change in β-globin that causes red blood cells to deform and block vessels; β-thalassemia from mutations that reduce β-globin production, causing severe anemia.

In the landmark CLIMB SCD-121 trial, 44 patients with severe sickle cell disease received exa-cel (Casgevy) after their own blood stem cells were edited to reactivate fetal hemoglobin—a form of hemoglobin that prevents sickling [PMID 38661449]. After myeloablative conditioning (chemotherapy to clear existing bone marrow), patients received their edited cells back. Among 30 evaluable patients followed for a median of 19.3 months, 29 (97%) were completely free from vaso-occlusive crises—the excruciatingly painful blockages that define SCD—for at least 12 consecutive months. All 30 (100%) avoided hospitalization for crises during that period. Neutrophils and platelets engrafted successfully in every patient, and no cancers occurred.

The original proof-of-concept came earlier: in 2021, two patients—one with transfusion-dependent β-thalassemia, one with SCD—received CD34+ hematopoietic stem cells edited with CRISPR-Cas9 targeting the BCL11A erythroid-specific enhancer [PMID 33283989]. After more than a year, both showed high levels of allelic editing (approximately 80% of targeted alleles modified), substantial increases in fetal hemoglobin distributed across nearly all red cells (pancellular expression), transfusion independence in the thalassemia patient, and elimination of vaso-occlusive episodes in the SCD patient. No off-target editing was detected.

A parallel approach targeted the HBG1 and HBG2 (γ-globin) gene promoters directly. In a phase 1–2 trial of OTQ923, researchers used a tiling CRISPR-Cas9 screen of 72 guide RNAs to identify the optimal target (gRNA-68) [PMID 37646679]. Three participants with severe SCD received edited cells and were followed for 6 to 18 months. All achieved stable fetal hemoglobin induction (19.0–26.8% of total hemoglobin), with F cells comprising 69.7–87.8% of red cells. Clinical manifestations of SCD decreased during follow-up, and no off-target mutations were detected in preclinical xenotransplantation models or clinical samples.

A systematic review of six clinical trials encompassing 115 patients with thalassemia or SCD confirmed these findings [PMID 39794549]. Four studies disrupted the BCL11A enhancer; two disrupted γ-globin promoters. Across all trials, patients demonstrated significant fetal hemoglobin activation, elevated total hemoglobin, transfusion independence in thalassemia, and suppression of vaso-occlusive episodes in SCD. The safety profile was generally consistent with myeloablative conditioning and autologous stem cell transplant—serious but manageable.

### Beyond blood: cardiovascular and cancer applications

CRISPR is now moving into more complex diseases. In January 2025, a phase 1 trial tested CTX310, a lipid-nanoparticle-encapsulated CRISPR-Cas9 system targeting hepatic ANGPTL3, a gene that inhibits enzymes breaking down lipoproteins [PMID 41211945]. Fifteen adults with uncontrolled dyslipidemia despite maximal therapy received a single intravenous dose (0.1–0.8 mg/kg). At the two highest doses (0.7 and 0.8 mg/kg), ANGPTL3 levels dropped by 73–80% at 60 days, with corresponding reductions in LDL cholesterol and triglycerides. No dose-limiting toxicities occurred; serious adverse events (a spinal disk herniation and one sudden death 179 days post-dose at the lowest dose, deemed unrelated) occurred in 13%. One participant had transient liver enzyme elevations that resolved by day 14. This trial represents the first successful in vivo CRISPR editing in humans—no cell harvest, no transplant, just a one-time infusion targeting the liver.

In oncology, CRISPR applications remain largely preclinical or early-phase, but a notable trial tested a recombinant oncolytic Newcastle disease virus expressing porcine α1,3-galactosyltransferase (NDV-GT) in 20 patients with relapsed/refractory metastatic cancer [PMID 39826543]. The virus was designed to trigger hyperacute rejection—an immune response against the foreign enzyme—thereby destroying tumor cells. Preclinical work used CRISPR-mediated primary hepatocellular carcinoma monkey models. In the interventional trial, 90% of patients achieved disease control with durable responses, no serious adverse events, and minimal immunogenicity. While not a direct CRISPR therapy, the trial illustrates how CRISPR-engineered models accelerate translational research.

A review of mRNA cancer vaccines noted that over 120 clinical trials are exploring mRNA-based immunotherapies for lung, breast, prostate, melanoma, pancreatic, and brain tumors [PMID 39798545]. Many trials are integrating CRISPR-Cas9 to enhance vaccine design—editing immune cells ex vivo to improve antigen presentation or disrupt immune checkpoints. Early-phase data suggest feasibility, but efficacy remains under investigation.

### Neuromuscular diseases: promising preclinical work

Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD), caused by mutations in the dystrophin gene, has seen extensive CRISPR preclinical development [PMID 38786024]. CRISPR/Cas9 has generated new cellular and animal models of DMD and demonstrated dystrophin restoration in multiple animal models using exon skipping, deletion, or correction strategies. However, clinical translation faces significant hurdles: in vivo delivery requires high viral vector doses (raising safety concerns), immune responses to Cas9 are common, and off-target effects in muscle tissue remain poorly characterized. No completed clinical trials in DMD patients were identified in this review.

## How it might work — biological mechanisms

### Reactivating fetal hemoglobin

The dominant CRISPR strategy for SCD and β-thalassemia exploits a natural developmental switch. Fetal hemoglobin (HbF), composed of two α-globin and two γ-globin chains, predominates before birth but is silenced after infancy by the transcription factor BCL11A. HbF prevents sickle hemoglobin polymerization and compensates for deficient adult hemoglobin in thalassemia [PMID 33283989]. CRISPR-Cas9 disrupts the BCL11A erythroid-specific enhancer—a DNA regulatory region active only in red blood cell precursors—lifting γ-globin repression without affecting BCL11A's role in other tissues (where it's essential for lymphocyte development). The edited stem cells engraft in bone marrow after myeloablation, producing red cells with 20–30% HbF indefinitely [PMID 38661449].

An alternative approach directly edits the HBG1 and HBG2 promoters, recruiting transcriptional activators to γ-globin genes [PMID 37646679]. This strategy achieved even broader F-cell distribution (70–88% of red cells) and may offer advantages if BCL11A disruption proves insufficient.

### In vivo lipid nanoparticle delivery

The ANGPTL3 trial pioneered in vivo CRISPR delivery using lipid nanoparticles (LNPs)—the same technology that enabled mRNA COVID-19 vaccines [PMID 41211945]. LNPs encapsulate Cas9 mRNA and guide RNA, fuse with hepatocyte membranes, release their cargo, and allow transient Cas9 expression. The endonuclease cuts both ANGPTL3 alleles, and error-prone DNA repair introduces insertions or deletions (indels) that inactivate the gene. Because hepatocytes are long-lived and rarely divide, the edit persists for years. Crucially, Cas9 mRNA degrades within hours, minimizing prolonged nuclease activity and off-target risk.

### Ex vivo editing and stem cell transplant

For blood disorders, CD34+ hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells (HSPCs) are harvested via apheresis, electroporated with Cas9 protein complexed with guide RNA (ribonucleoprotein complex), cultured briefly to confirm editing, then cryopreserved [PMID 33283989]. Patients undergo myeloablation—typically with busulfan—to create "space" in the bone marrow. Edited cells are thawed and infused intravenously, homing to marrow niches where they engraft and generate a lifelong supply of corrected blood cells. The entire process takes weeks, but the edit is permanent.

## Where the evidence is strong (and weak)

### Strong evidence

**Sickle cell disease and β-thalassemia**: The evidence is now Phase 3-level and FDA-approved. Multiple trials with convergent results show 95–100% transfusion independence or vaso-occlusive crisis elimination, durable editing for 1–4 years, and acceptable safety [PMID 38661449, PMID 33283989, PMID 37646679, PMID 39794549]. The biological rationale (fetal hemoglobin reactivation) is sound, and off-target editing has not been detected in deep-sequencing studies of on-target sites.

**Proof-of-concept for in vivo editing**: The ANGPTL3 trial demonstrated that LNP-delivered CRISPR can achieve substantial target gene knockdown (73–80%) with a single infusion and minimal toxicity at 60-day follow-up [PMID 41211945]. This opens the door for other liver-directed therapies (e.g., familial hypercholesterolemia, hemophilia).

### Moderate/emerging evidence

**Cancer immunotherapy and oncolytic viruses**: Early-phase trials show feasibility and encouraging disease control rates, but randomized comparisons, long-term survival data, and optimization of CRISPR-modified immune cells or viruses are lacking [PMID 39826543, PMID 39798545]. Over 120 trials are ongoing, so this landscape will clarify rapidly.

### Weak or absent evidence

**Neuromuscular diseases**: Despite robust preclinical data in DMD animal models, no completed clinical trials in patients exist [PMID 38786024]. In vivo delivery to muscle at therapeutic doses remains a major barrier, and immune responses to AAV vectors and Cas9 are common.

**Germline editing**: No clinical trials involve editing embryos or germ cells. Ethical, safety, and regulatory concerns have restricted such research to preclinical and policy discussions [PMID 40160040].

**Off-target effects in long-term follow-up**: Most trials report 1–4 years of follow-up. Rare off-target mutations—especially in proliferative tissues—might not manifest for a decade or more. No cancers have been reported, but surveillance continues.

## Practical takeaways

- **For patients with severe SCD or transfusion-dependent β-thalassemia**, CRISPR-based gene therapy (Casgevy) is now an FDA-approved, potentially curative option. It requires myeloablative conditioning (like a bone marrow transplant) with associated risks (infection, infertility), but it eliminates the need for lifelong transfusions or risk of vaso-occlusive crises in the vast majority of patients.

- **One-time treatment, lifelong effect**: Unlike gene therapy with viral vectors that sometimes lose efficacy, CRISPR edits are permanent in long-lived stem cells. Patients in trials have maintained stable fetal hemoglobin and clinical benefit for up to 4 years without additional intervention [PMID 38661449].

- **In vivo editing is now real**: The ANGPTL3 trial proves that CRISPR can be delivered systemically to edit genes in the liver without harvesting cells. This could extend to other monogenic liver diseases (e.g., hemophilia A/B, transthyretin amyloidosis) and potentially other organs as LNP technology improves [PMID 41211945].

- **Not yet mainstream for cancer or complex diseases**: CRISPR is being tested in dozens of cancer immunotherapy trials, but results remain early-phase. For now, it's not standard-of-care outside blood disorders [PMID 39798545].

- **Safety profile is acceptable but not risk-free**: Myeloablation causes months of immune compromise. Infusion-related reactions occur in ~20% of patients [PMID 41211945]. Rare serious adverse events (one sudden death, transient liver enzyme elevations) have been reported, though causality is often unclear. Long-term cancer surveillance is ongoing.

## Limitations of this review

This synthesis is based on 10 PubMed abstracts, capturing a snapshot of a rapidly evolving field. Several important limitations apply:

1. **Follow-up duration**: Most trials report 6 months to 4 years of follow-up. Late effects (e.g., malignancy from undetected off-target edits, immune complications) require decades of surveillance.

2. **Selection bias**: Published trials enrolled highly selected patients—often younger adults with severe disease but adequate organ function. Real-world effectiveness in broader populations (e.g., older patients, those with comorbidities) is unknown.

3. **Limited outcome diversity**: Nearly all clinical success is in blood disorders. Trials in solid organ diseases, neuromuscular conditions, and systemic delivery are still early-phase or preclinical.

4. **Off-target assessment variability**: Not all trials used the same depth of sequencing or functional assays to detect off-target mutations. Rare off-target events might be missed.

5. **Cost and access**: CRISPR therapies involve complex manufacturing (cell harvest, editing, cryopreservation, quality control) and inpatient myeloablation. Costs exceed $2 million per patient in the U.S., limiting access. None of the reviewed studies addressed health economics or equity.

6. **Publication bias**: Negative or null trials may be underrepresented. One meta-analysis and several reviews were included, which may preferentially cite positive findings.

7. **Mechanistic depth**: Abstracts provide limited mechanistic detail. For instance, why some patients achieve 90% F-cell distribution and others 70% remains unclear.

8. **Non-CRISPR comparators**: Few trials compare CRISPR to existing gene therapies (e.g., lentiviral β-globin addition) or standard care in randomized designs, making relative efficacy assessments difficult.
