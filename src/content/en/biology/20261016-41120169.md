## The key finding

A 2025 systematic review of 13 studies found that psychosocial interventions combining diabetes education with empowerment strategies produced small to moderate reductions in diabetes distress among culturally diverse populations—but only in the short term. Most programs ran for four months or less, and nearly all adaptations stopped at translating materials rather than addressing deeper cultural beliefs about diabetes. Peer-led interventions, surprisingly, showed limited effectiveness for psychological outcomes, and family-based approaches produced mixed results. Diabetes distress—the emotional burden of managing a chronic condition—runs notably higher in minority groups, yet evidence for sustained, culturally nuanced support remains sparse.

## What the study looked like

Researchers conducted a scoping review following the Arksey and O'Malley framework, searching five major databases (PubMed, PsycINFO, Cochrane Library, CINAHL, Web of Science) for peer-reviewed studies published between 2013 and 2024. They included studies involving adults from culturally diverse groups diagnosed with diabetes who participated in psychosocial interventions. While the search did not restrict by diabetes type, all 13 included studies enrolled adults with type 2 diabetes. The review excluded research on caregivers, healthcare providers, Native and Indigenous groups, and purely physiological lifestyle interventions. Studies could be from any country and use any research design as long as they were published in English. The reviewers extracted details on participant demographics, intervention features, and outcomes including satisfaction and dropout rates, then synthesized findings thematically by intervention type.

## Why researchers think this happened

The review suggests that empowerment-based interventions may work better because they actively engage participants in problem-solving and self-management decision-making, building confidence alongside knowledge. Longer empowerment programs showed more gradual but potentially more durable changes in distress levels. Peer-led interventions may have underperformed because facilitators lacked formal training in addressing complex psychological needs, or because shared cultural background alone cannot substitute for therapeutic skill. The disappointing results for many family-based approaches may reflect insufficient adaptation to specific family structures and dynamics within different cultural contexts. The authors note that most interventions translated materials into participants' languages but failed to address underlying cultural beliefs about diabetes causation, stigma, or family roles in care—factors known to influence distress in minority communities.

## How to read this carefully

This scoping review included only 13 studies, a modest evidence base that limits generalizability. The authors flagged notable gaps: South Asian and Middle Eastern populations received little attention, and most interventions were short-term with no follow-up data to assess whether benefits persisted beyond a few months. Cultural adaptation typically meant language translation rather than deeper modifications to intervention content or delivery. The review included various study designs without meta-analysis, so effect sizes were not quantitatively compared across interventions. The lack of longitudinal data means we cannot know whether distress reductions translate into sustained improvements in clinical outcomes like glycemic control or quality of life. Because all included studies focused on type 2 diabetes, findings may not apply to people with type 1 diabetes or other forms.

## What this means for everyday life

If you or a loved one lives with type 2 diabetes and comes from a culturally diverse background, these findings suggest that programs emphasizing empowerment—where you actively participate in setting goals and problem-solving—may help reduce the emotional burden more than peer support groups alone. However, given that most interventions were brief, it may be worth seeking ongoing support rather than one-time workshops. The review highlights that simply offering materials in your language may not address cultural beliefs or family expectations around diabetes care. When considering a diabetes support program, ask whether it goes beyond translation to include discussion of cultural attitudes, family dynamics, and stigma. The evidence base remains limited, so approach new programs as one piece of a broader self-care strategy rather than a definitive solution.
