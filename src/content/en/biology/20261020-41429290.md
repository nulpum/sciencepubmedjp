## The key finding

A 2026 scoping review of 52 sensor-based devices designed to automatically detect eating and drinking occasions found that none are yet feasible for use in real-world nutrition practice. The review evaluated wrist-worn, neck-worn, ear-worn, and glasses-type devices against six practical criteria. Battery life emerged as the most common limitation, with 43 out of 52 devices (83%) failing to provide adequate power for continuous daily use. Real-time processing capability was missing in 37 devices (71%), and 31 devices (60%) could not detect both eating and drinking behaviors—critical for comprehensive dietary monitoring.

## What the study looked like

Researchers conducted a scoping review following standardized methodology, searching for studies published between January 2022 and September 2025 that tested sensor-based devices for detecting food or beverage intake. The final analysis included 50 studies describing 52 different devices: 19 wrist-worn (like smartwatches), 8 neck-worn, 7 ear-worn, 7 glasses-type, 6 in an "other" category (including chairs and smart tables), and 5 that combined multiple sensor positions. Each device was evaluated against six feasibility criteria: at least 80% detection accuracy, freedom in food and beverage selection during testing, social acceptability and comfort for everyday wear, long battery life, real-time detection capability, and ability to detect both eating and drinking occasions.

## Why researchers think this happened

The persistent gap between laboratory performance and real-world readiness stems from fundamental engineering trade-offs, according to the study authors. Sensors capable of high accuracy often require substantial computational power, which drains batteries quickly. Many devices achieve impressive detection rates in controlled settings with limited food variety but struggle when faced with the diversity of real-world eating behaviors—snacking, drinking different beverages, eating while walking, or consuming mixed meals. The authors note this represents a follow-up to their 2021 review, which also concluded devices weren't ready for practice. However, they observed meaningful progress in two areas: newer devices show improved social acceptability through less conspicuous designs, and computational efficiency has advanced, suggesting battery life challenges may be solvable in coming years.

## How to read this carefully

This review specifically assessed feasibility for nutrition practice, not laboratory research potential. A device might perform excellently in a study with 20 participants eating standardized meals but fail in everyday scenarios with thousands of food types and eating contexts. The six criteria represent an intentionally high bar—necessary for replacing current dietary assessment methods but possibly too strict for devices meant to supplement rather than replace traditional approaches. The review also couldn't account for unpublished commercial developments or devices still in early testing phases. Additionally, "feasibility" here focuses on technical performance, not cost, data privacy, or integration with existing healthcare systems—all critical for actual adoption.

## What this means for everyday life

For now, the vision of a wearable device that automatically tracks your eating throughout the day—eliminating food diaries or photo logging—remains future technology rather than current reality. If you're considering nutrition apps or wearables, recognize that existing tools still rely heavily on manual input or highly simplified detection. The progress noted in this review suggests we're moving in the right direction: future devices may combine smartwatch convenience with reliable intake detection, potentially enabling your phone to prompt healthier choices right when you're deciding what to eat. Until then, traditional food logging, though imperfect and burdensome, remains the most practical option for anyone working with a dietitian or tracking intake for health reasons.
