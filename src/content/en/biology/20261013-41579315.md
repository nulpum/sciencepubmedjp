## The key finding

Researchers analyzed 32 studies to identify what makes people spiritually resilient during disasters and emergencies. They found that spiritual resilience clusters around three core domains: adherence to rituals (such as prayer or meditation practices), personal growth (finding meaning and transformation through adversity), and a sense of social responsibility (feeling connected to and accountable for one's community). This 2026 systematic review represents the first attempt to categorize spiritual resilience indicators in a way that could enable standardized measurement during crises.

## What the study looked like

This was a systematic review—a study that gathers and analyzes existing research rather than collecting new data. Researchers searched English and Persian-language databases for studies examining spiritual resilience in disaster and emergency contexts. Out of the literature screened, 32 articles met inclusion criteria. The team extracted indicators of spiritual resilience mentioned across these studies and grouped them thematically. The review did not specify participant demographics or disaster types in detail, as it synthesized findings across diverse populations and crisis scenarios reported in the included studies.

## Why researchers think this happened

The authors propose that spiritual resilience during disasters operates through interconnected pathways. Adherence to rituals likely provides structure, continuity, and comfort when external circumstances feel chaotic—rituals anchor people to something stable. Personal growth reflects the human capacity to find meaning even in suffering, a concept aligned with Viktor Frankl's logotherapy and post-traumatic growth literature. A sense of social responsibility taps into collective coping: feeling part of something larger than oneself may buffer against despair and motivate prosocial behavior during crises. The researchers note that these three domains emerged consistently across culturally diverse studies, suggesting they may represent universal facets of spiritual resilience rather than culture-specific phenomena.

## How to read this carefully

This review synthesized existing studies, so its findings are only as strong as the quality of those 32 articles—and the paper does not detail their methodological rigor or sample sizes. Systematic reviews can reveal patterns, but they cannot prove causation. We do not know whether spiritual practices *cause* resilience or whether resilient people are simply more likely to engage in such practices. The inclusion of both English and Persian sources broadens cultural scope, but the review may still miss research published in other languages or unpublished findings. Additionally, "spiritual resilience" is inherently subjective and may mean different things across religions, cultures, and individuals. Standardized tools developed from these indicators will need rigorous validation before use in real-world disaster response.

## What this means for everyday life

If you are thinking about your own preparedness for crises—whether natural disasters, public health emergencies, or personal upheavals—this research suggests that spiritual practices may play a role in resilience. That does not mean adopting any particular religion, but rather considering what rituals ground you (whether meditation, journaling, or community gatherings), how you make meaning from hardship, and how connected you feel to others. For disaster response organizations, these findings highlight the potential value of integrating spiritual and psychological support into crisis interventions. Given this, it might be worth reflecting on which of these three domains—ritual, growth, responsibility—feels most accessible to you, and exploring practices that strengthen it before the next crisis arrives.
