## What we are looking at

Multiple myeloma is a cancer of the plasma cells in the bone marrow, and over the last two decades it has shifted from a rapidly fatal diagnosis to a chronic condition that many patients live with for many years. That change in survival has made quality-of-life questions, including cognitive function, far more important than they used to be.

A recurrent theme that patients raise in clinic and in patient-reported outcome studies is a sense of "brain fog": trouble following conversations, slower thinking, difficulty juggling tasks, and a feeling that the mind is a step behind where it used to be. In the hematology literature this is usually described under broader terms such as cancer-related cognitive impairment (CRCI) or chemotherapy-related cognitive impairment, sometimes called "chemo-fog". This review summarizes what the systematic review literature on PubMed actually shows when the question is narrowed to multiple myeloma specifically.

The clinical context matters. Multiple myeloma treatment typically involves a long sequence of regimens: induction with proteasome inhibitors (such as bortezomib and carfilzomib), immunomodulatory drugs (lenalidomide, pomalidomide), monoclonal antibodies (daratumumab, isatuximab), high-dose melphalan with autologous stem cell transplantation for eligible patients, maintenance therapy, and later-line options including bispecific antibodies and CAR T-cell therapy. Each of these touches the central nervous system differently, and many patients experience several of them over a decade of disease.

## The evidence — what studies actually found

Recent systematic reviews and meta-analyses examining cognitive function in multiple myeloma consistently reach several conclusions.

**Cognitive deficits are common and measurable.** Across neuropsychological testing studies, a substantial proportion of myeloma patients—often reported in the range of roughly one third to one half, depending on timing and measurement—score below age-adjusted norms on at least one cognitive domain. The most consistently affected domains are attention, processing speed, working memory, and executive function. Verbal learning and long-term memory can also be affected, but language and crystallized knowledge tend to be relatively preserved.

**Deficits are present early, not only late.** Even at diagnosis, before any treatment is given, a meaningful fraction of patients show lower-than-expected performance on cognitive testing. This is important because it means not all of the burden can be attributed to chemotherapy. Disease-related factors—anemia, high cytokine load, renal dysfunction, hypercalcemia, and in some patients central nervous system involvement—can all affect cognition before the first dose of therapy.

**Treatment adds further burden, especially around transplantation.** Studies that have followed patients longitudinally through high-dose melphalan and autologous stem cell transplantation generally find a measurable decline in several cognitive domains in the weeks to months after transplant, with partial recovery over the subsequent year. Full recovery to pre-treatment levels, however, is not universal. A clinically relevant subset of patients continues to show deficits one to two years out from transplant, especially in processing speed and executive function.

**Patient-reported cognitive problems do not line up perfectly with test scores.** One of the most robust findings across studies is a dissociation between how patients describe their own cognition ("I cannot remember names any more", "I lose track of what I am doing") and what formal neuropsychological testing shows. Many patients who perform within the normal range on tests still report meaningful subjective difficulties, and vice versa. This is seen across cancer types but is particularly prominent in myeloma, probably because the baseline age of patients is high (median around 70 at diagnosis) and because treatment extends over many years, blending into normal aging.

**Quality-of-life impact is substantial.** Studies using validated instruments such as the EORTC QLQ-CIPN20 (for neuropathy) and EORTC QLQ-C30 (for general quality of life) consistently show that cognitive and neurological complaints are among the top-ranked concerns for myeloma patients, alongside fatigue and pain. Cognitive difficulties affect return to work, driving, financial management, and social participation—domains that matter deeply in a disease where many patients live for a decade or more after diagnosis.

## How it might work — biological mechanisms

Multiple plausible mechanisms likely contribute together, rather than a single cause.

**Direct effects of the disease.** Multiple myeloma is a highly inflammatory condition. Monoclonal plasma cells and the surrounding bone-marrow microenvironment produce large amounts of cytokines, in particular interleukin-6 (IL-6), which has been linked to depressive symptoms and cognitive slowing in many other settings. Renal impairment, which is common in myeloma, can lead to uremic cognitive dysfunction. Severe anemia reduces oxygen delivery to the brain. Hypercalcemia, when present, produces well-known neuropsychiatric symptoms including confusion and slowing of thought.

**Chemotherapy and immunomodulatory drug effects.** High-dose melphalan used in autologous stem cell transplantation is cytotoxic to dividing cells throughout the body, including neural stem cells in brain regions such as the hippocampus, which supports memory. Immunomodulatory drugs such as lenalidomide act partly by modulating cytokine production and T-cell function; their long-term use is associated with fatigue, somnolence, and sometimes patient-reported cognitive slowing, though direct neurotoxicity appears modest. Proteasome inhibitors, particularly bortezomib, are best known for causing peripheral neuropathy, but there is ongoing investigation into whether central nervous system effects contribute to cognitive symptoms in some patients.

**Peripheral neuropathy and cognitive symptoms share pathways.** Treatment-induced peripheral neuropathy and chemotherapy-related cognitive impairment are not the same thing, but they correlate. Both involve inflammatory signaling, oxidative stress, and in some cases mitochondrial dysfunction. Patients with more severe neuropathy tend, on average, to report more cognitive complaints as well, suggesting shared vulnerability rather than simple coincidence.

**Secondary factors: sleep, mood, pain, and medication burden.** By the time patients have been in treatment for a year or two, they are often on multiple medications beyond the cancer regimen itself: opioid analgesics for bone pain, anti-emetics, sleep aids, antidepressants, and anti-anxiety medications. Each of these can produce measurable cognitive effects, and together they create a cumulative burden that is hard to separate from "pure" chemotherapy effects. Pain and poor sleep each independently reduce cognitive performance, so improving these can improve cognition without any direct effect on the disease.

**Older age at baseline.** The median age at diagnosis of multiple myeloma is around 70, which means that many patients are already navigating normal age-related changes in processing speed and working memory. Treatment-related cognitive effects stack on top of this baseline, which is one reason why younger myeloma patients (those diagnosed in their 50s) often report proportionally larger subjective changes—they are comparing themselves to a higher pre-illness baseline.

## Where the evidence is strong (and weak)

**Strong evidence exists for:**

- The existence of measurable cognitive deficits in a substantial fraction of multiple myeloma patients, at diagnosis and during treatment
- Processing speed, attention, and executive function being the most consistently affected domains
- A measurable dip in cognition in the months after autologous stem cell transplantation, with partial but incomplete recovery
- Substantial impact on quality of life, return to work, and daily function
- The relevance of fatigue, pain, mood, and medication burden as contributors beyond chemotherapy itself

**Evidence is more uncertain for:**

- The precise long-term trajectory in modern treatment era, where new agents (bispecifics, CAR T, continuous maintenance) have changed the treatment landscape and older studies may not fully reflect current practice
- Whether specific agents (for example, lenalidomide maintenance versus observation) produce meaningfully different cognitive trajectories at the group level
- Which interventions—cognitive rehabilitation, exercise, mindfulness-based programs, pharmacologic approaches—reliably improve cognitive outcomes for myeloma patients specifically, as opposed to cancer patients in general
- The role of clonal hematopoiesis and shared genetic risk between myeloma and cognitive decline, an active area of research

**Evidence is very limited for:**

- Whether cognitive screening should be routine in all myeloma patients, and if so with which instruments
- Whether early identification and intervention change long-term outcomes
- The impact of newer modalities such as CAR T-cell therapy on cognitive function, where follow-up durations remain short

## What patients and clinicians can take from this

For patients, the single most useful message from the evidence is that cognitive symptoms in multiple myeloma are real, measurable, and acknowledged in the research literature. They are not a sign of weakness or imagination. At the same time, the trajectory is not uniformly downhill: many patients recover a meaningful share of their cognitive function once active treatment ends, and some of the biggest wins come from addressing treatable contributors—pain, sleep, mood, anemia, and medication review.

For clinicians, the evidence supports treating patient-reported cognitive complaints seriously, actively screening for and addressing contributing factors, and considering referral to neuropsychology or cognitive rehabilitation services where available, particularly around transplantation and in patients returning to work. For investigators, the open questions sit mainly in the modern treatment era and in intervention trials with cognitive endpoints, which remain under-represented relative to the clinical importance of the problem.
