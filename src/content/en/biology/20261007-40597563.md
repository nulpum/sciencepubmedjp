## The key finding

A 2025 review defines postbiotics as "preparations of inanimate microorganisms and/or their components that confer a health benefit to the host," positioning them as a new category separate from traditional probiotics. Unlike probiotics, which require living microbes to reach the gut, postbiotics deliver health-promoting substances from already-inactivated bacteria or their cellular parts. The authors argue that choosing between probiotics and postbiotics should depend on the specific microorganism strain, the target population, and individual health needs — not on the assumption that "live is always better."

## What the study looked like

This is a narrative review of the scientific literature on postbiotic administration, published in 2025 in *Food Research International*. The authors systematically compared evidence from studies using live probiotic microorganisms versus their inactivated (postbiotic) counterparts. They examined clinical, nutritional, and mechanistic research across various populations and health conditions. The review also explored emerging genomic and metabolomic ('omics) technologies as tools for identifying which microbial strains might work best as postbiotics. Rather than conducting new experiments, the researchers synthesized findings from existing studies to map the current state of postbiotic science and highlight gaps in knowledge and regulation.

## Why researchers think this happened

The authors suggest that postbiotics work through mechanisms distinct from live probiotics. While probiotics exert effects by colonizing or temporarily inhabiting the gut and producing beneficial compounds in real time, postbiotics deliver pre-formed bioactive molecules — such as cell wall fragments, enzymes, metabolites, or signaling peptides — that can interact directly with the host's immune system or gut lining. This means postbiotics may offer advantages in situations where live bacteria are impractical: they are stable at room temperature, survive stomach acid without special coatings, and pose no risk of infection in immunocompromised individuals. The review notes that 'omics technologies can help researchers screen microbial genomes to predict which strains will produce the most beneficial postbiotic components, accelerating the identification of effective formulations.

## How to read this carefully

This is a review article, not a clinical trial, so it synthesizes existing evidence rather than testing postbiotics directly. The authors acknowledge a major limitation: the postbiotic field lacks clear regulatory definitions and standardized testing protocols, making it difficult to compare products or replicate findings across studies. Because postbiotic formulations vary widely — different strains, different inactivation methods, different component mixtures — results from one preparation may not generalize to another. The review does not rank specific postbiotic products or recommend doses, and much of the cited evidence comes from preclinical or small-scale human studies. Readers should be cautious about assuming all postbiotics are equally effective or that they can replace probiotics in every context.

## What this means for everyday life

Given this emerging evidence, consumers may soon encounter postbiotic supplements alongside traditional probiotics on store shelves. If you have difficulty storing probiotic supplements in the refrigerator, travel frequently, or have a weakened immune system, postbiotics might offer a more practical or safer option — though you should consult a healthcare provider before switching. The review suggests that neither probiotics nor postbiotics are universally superior; the choice depends on individual health goals and the specific microbial strain in question. As regulation catches up with science, clearer labeling and quality standards may help guide informed decisions. For now, it is worth staying curious about this evolving category and recognizing that the "live versus dead" microbe debate is more nuanced than it first appears.
