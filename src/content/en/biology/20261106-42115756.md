## The key finding

A 2026 review in *Nature Reviews Genetics* reveals that ageing is fundamentally an evolutionary byproduct: natural selection weakens dramatically after reproductive age, creating a "selection shadow" where deleterious mutations that act late in life can accumulate without being weeded out. Additionally, genetic variants that boost early-life fitness often persist even when they cause harm later — a phenomenon called antagonistic pleiotropy. This framework explains puzzling patterns across biology: why lifespan varies 100-fold among vertebrates, why conserved longevity pathways exist across species from yeast to humans, and why age-related diseases like cancer and neurodegeneration share overlapping genetic architectures.

## What the study looked like

This is a comprehensive scientific review synthesizing decades of research across evolutionary biology, comparative genomics, and human genetics. The authors examined evidence from multiple sources: cross-species comparisons spanning the entire tree of life (from single-celled organisms to mammals), large-scale human genome-wide association studies (GWAS) tracking genetic variants linked to ageing traits, and multi-omics datasets measuring molecular biomarkers of ageing (epigenetics, proteomics, metabolomics). Rather than a single experiment, the review integrates findings from observational studies, comparative genomic analyses of long-lived versus short-lived species, and population-level genetic studies to test evolutionary predictions about how and why ageing evolves. The scope includes both model organisms used in laboratory longevity research and real-world human populations experiencing modern extended lifespans.

## Why researchers think this happened

The core evolutionary mechanism is straightforward: natural selection acts most powerfully on traits affecting reproduction and early survival. Once organisms pass reproductive age, selection pressure drops sharply because genes active only in old age have minimal impact on evolutionary fitness — they aren't passed to offspring as efficiently. This creates two pathways to ageing. First, mutation accumulation: harmful mutations that manifest only late in life escape selection's filter and build up over generations. Second, antagonistic pleiotropy: genes that enhance early-life reproduction or growth can have negative side effects decades later, but selection favors the early benefit. The authors cite evidence that the same molecular pathways regulating growth and reproduction in youth (like insulin/IGF-1 signaling) drive age-related decline when dysregulated later. Comparative genomics shows that long-lived species like naked mole rats and bowhead whales have evolved specific genetic modifications to these conserved pathways, supporting the idea that lifespan is tunable through evolutionary mechanisms rather than fixed by fundamental biological limits.

## How to read this carefully

This is a theoretical review synthesizing existing research, not a single experimental study producing new data. Evolutionary explanations describe *why* ageing exists across populations and species, but they don't predict individual variation in human healthspan with precision. The 100-fold lifespan variation mentioned spans all vertebrates (from fish living months to whales living centuries), not variation within humans. While the evolutionary framework is well-supported, translating these insights into interventions remains challenging: knowing that a pathway was optimized for youth doesn't automatically reveal how to safely modify it in old age. Human genetic studies cited often show associations (genetic variants *linked to* longevity), not causation, and effect sizes for individual variants are typically small. The review acknowledges that modern humans now live far beyond the lifespans selection optimized for, meaning we're navigating uncharted biological territory.

## What this means for everyday life

This evolutionary perspective reframes how we might think about ageing and health. If our bodies are genuinely optimized for early life at the expense of late life, it suggests that age-related diseases aren't separate conditions but interconnected consequences of the same underlying evolutionary trade-offs — which explains why interventions targeting fundamental ageing processes (like caloric restriction or mTOR inhibition in animal studies) can simultaneously affect multiple age-related conditions. For individuals, this underscores that many "diseases of ageing" may be partially preventable through lifestyle factors that modulate these ancient pathways, though specific recommendations require clinical evidence beyond evolutionary theory. The finding that conserved longevity pathways exist across species offers cautious optimism: ageing isn't an immutable biological wall but a process shaped by evolutionary constraints that, in principle, could be modified. However, given the complexity and the fact that selection didn't optimize us for longevity beyond reproductive years, any healthspan extension efforts should be approached carefully and grounded in rigorous evidence rather than evolutionary speculation alone.
