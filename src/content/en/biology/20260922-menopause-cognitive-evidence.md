## What we're looking at

More than one billion women worldwide will experience menopause during their lifetimes, and dementia disproportionately affects women. Because estrogen levels plummet during the menopausal transition, researchers have long wondered whether hormone replacement might protect the aging brain. The biological rationale seems sound: estrogen receptors are abundant in brain regions critical for memory, and animal studies consistently show that estrogen can protect neurons from damage [PMID 9496988]. Yet decades of human research have produced a confusing tangle of results—some suggesting benefit, others showing harm, and many finding no effect at all.

This review synthesizes evidence from multiple meta-analyses and systematic reviews spanning more than two decades to answer a deceptively simple question: Does menopause hormone therapy (MHT)—also called hormone replacement therapy or HRT—help maintain cognitive function or prevent dementia in postmenopausal women?

## The evidence — what studies actually found

The most recent and comprehensive analysis offers a sobering answer. A 2025 systematic review and meta-analysis examined ten studies encompassing over one million women and found no significant association between MHT use and risk of mild cognitive impairment or dementia [PMID 41448220]. Subgroup analyses examining timing of initiation, duration of use, and type of hormone therapy all showed no significant effects—neither protective nor harmful.

This null finding actually represents the culmination of an evolving evidence base. An early meta-analysis from 1998 painted a more optimistic picture, suggesting a 29% decreased risk of developing dementia among estrogen users based on ten observational studies [PMID 9496988]. However, the authors noted substantial heterogeneity in study findings and cautioned against clinical recommendations given methodological limitations.

Randomized controlled trials (RCTs)—considered the gold standard of medical evidence—have been less encouraging. A 2008 Cochrane review of 24 trials involving 10,114 women found that both estrogen-only therapy (ERT) and combined hormone therapy (HRT with estrogen plus progestagen) failed to prevent cognitive impairment after four to five years of treatment [PMID 18254016]. More troubling, analyses suggested these treatments might even adversely affect cognitive function, with negative effects emerging after one year for ERT and after three to four years for combined HRT.

An earlier 2002 Cochrane review identified 15 trials but found little evidence of benefit for overall cognitive function [PMID 12137675]. The few positive effects detected—improvements in immediate recall, abstract reasoning, and processing speed—came from small studies in relatively young surgically menopausal women (average age 47) receiving high-dose intramuscular estradiol injections, all from a single research group.

A 2000 meta-analysis noted that effects of hormone therapy on cognition were "small and inconsistent," including modest enhancement of verbal memory, abstract reasoning, and information processing in healthy aging women [PMID 11113299]. Importantly, this analysis found that epidemiological studies showed larger effects than experimental studies—a pattern often indicating confounding bias rather than true treatment effects.

Duration of treatment emerged as a critical variable. A 2010 meta-analysis of 36 randomized trials found that longer studies showed more negative effects, while positive effects appeared mainly in short-term studies lasting less than four months [PMID 20202765]. Combined estrogen-progestagen therapy also negatively affected outcomes compared to estrogen alone.

## How it might work — biological mechanisms

The disconnect between promising preclinical data and disappointing clinical results demands explanation. At the cellular level, estrogens interact with multiple brain systems that govern memory and cognition. Estrogen promotes cholinergic and serotonergic activity in brain regions essential for memory formation, helps maintain neural circuitry, produces favorable changes in blood lipoproteins, and may prevent cerebral ischemia (reduced blood flow) [PMID 9496988].

Animal models consistently demonstrate neuroprotective effects of estrogen, including reduced brain damage in stroke models [PMID 20001955]. Estrogen receptors are densely distributed in the hippocampus and cortex—structures critical for learning and memory—providing a plausible anatomical basis for cognitive effects.

However, biological plausibility doesn't guarantee clinical benefit. The timing of estrogen exposure relative to menopause may be critical. Some researchers proposed a "critical window" or "window of opportunity" hypothesis: estrogen might protect neurons that are still healthy but fail to rescue or even harm neurons that have already undergone age-related changes. This could explain why hormone therapy initiated soon after menopause in observational studies appeared beneficial, while interventions in older postmenopausal women showed neutral or negative effects.

Yet the 2010 meta-analysis found that age at treatment initiation and time elapsed since menopause did not significantly affect outcomes—contrary to the window hypothesis [PMID 20202765]. Similarly, the presence or absence of menopausal symptoms didn't predict cognitive response.

The type of menopause may matter. Women who undergo surgical menopause (bilateral oophorectomy, or removal of both ovaries) before natural menopause experience an abrupt hormonal crash. Evidence suggests these women face increased risk of neurological disorders and cognitive impairment, particularly affecting global cognition and verbal memory [PMID 24942013]. For this group specifically, some data suggest hormone therapy may be beneficial if initiated close to the time of surgery and around the average age of natural menopause—though longer-term observational studies show negative effects after more than ten years of treatment.

## Where the evidence is strong (and weak)

The strength of current evidence lies in large-scale RCTs with adequate follow-up periods. These studies consistently fail to show cognitive benefit from hormone therapy in older postmenopausal women, and some suggest potential harm with longer duration or combined estrogen-progestagen formulations. The 2025 meta-analysis provides moderate-to-very-low certainty evidence that MHT neither increases nor decreases dementia risk in postmenopausal women [PMID 41448220].

The evidence is weakest in several critical areas. First, almost no studies have examined testosterone or other androgens despite their potential cognitive effects. Second, women with premature ovarian insufficiency (POI)—those who experience menopause before age 40—remain severely understudied [PMID 41448220]. This group may represent a unique case, as they lose hormonal support decades earlier than evolutionarily typical.

Third, where POI results from genetic disorders like Turner syndrome, observed cognitive deficits may reflect genetic factors rather than hormone deficiency per se [PMID 24942013]. Teasing apart genetic from hormonal contributions requires careful study design rarely achieved.

Fourth, most research has focused on oral conjugated equine estrogens (Premarin), the most widely prescribed formulation, which showed the least cognitive benefit in meta-analyses [PMID 11113299]. Different preparations (bioidentical estradiol), routes of administration (transdermal patches versus pills), and dosing regimens may produce different results. The 2008 Cochrane review noted that insufficient evidence exists to determine whether specific estrogen types, doses, or delivery methods benefit cognitive function [PMID 18254016].

Finally, whether estrogen therapy helps women who already have mild cognitive impairment remains uncertain. Three controlled studies using Premarin showed no benefit in preventing further decline in women with diagnosed Alzheimer's disease [PMID 11113299]. Duration of treatment may be crucial here, with benefits declining or even reversing over time in women with existing dementia.

A peculiar finding deserves mention: both healthy and demented women with lower educational attainment appeared to benefit most from hormone therapy in epidemiological studies [PMID 11113299]. This counter-intuitive observation suggests complex interactions between cognitive reserve, hormonal status, and treatment effects that warrant further investigation.

## Practical takeaways

For most postmenopausal women considering hormone therapy, cognitive protection should not factor into the decision. The evidence does not support using MHT to prevent dementia or maintain cognitive function. This aligns with current clinical guidance that hormone therapy decisions should rest on other considerations—primarily managing bothersome menopausal symptoms like hot flashes and night sweats [PMID 41448220].

Women experiencing early menopause before age 40 or those undergoing surgical menopause represent special cases requiring individualized discussion with healthcare providers. Current evidence suggests these women face elevated cognitive and neurological risks, and hormone therapy initiated near the time of menopause and continued until the typical age of natural menopause may offer some protection [PMID 24942013]. However, benefits of extended treatment beyond this period remain uncertain.

For women already taking hormone therapy for symptom management, duration matters. Short-term use (under four months) shows the most favorable cognitive profile in meta-analyses, while longer duration associates with more negative effects [PMID 20202765]. The type of regimen may also matter, with combined estrogen-progestagen therapy showing worse cognitive outcomes than estrogen alone.

Women with existing cognitive impairment or diagnosed Alzheimer's disease should not expect cognitive improvement from hormone therapy. Limited evidence suggests no benefit and possible harm in this population [PMID 11113299].

The persistent confusion around hormone therapy and cognition underscores an important principle: interventions that make biological sense and show promise in animal models don't always translate to human benefit. The human brain ages in complex ways that laboratory models cannot fully replicate.

## Limitations of this review

This synthesis has several limitations. First, it draws primarily from meta-analyses and systematic reviews rather than individual primary studies, which means we inherit the quality limitations and potential biases of those higher-level syntheses. The 2025 meta-analysis rated certainty of evidence as moderate to very low [PMID 41448220], reflecting concerns about study heterogeneity, risk of bias in observational studies, and inconsistent outcome measures across trials.

Second, many included trials were not originally designed to assess cognitive outcomes as primary endpoints. Cognitive testing was often a secondary measure, potentially underpowering statistical detection of subtle effects. Additionally, different studies used different cognitive assessment tools, making cross-study comparisons challenging.

Third, observational studies—which initially suggested benefit—are vulnerable to "healthy user bias." Women who choose to start hormone therapy tend to be healthier, wealthier, and better educated than those who don't. Even sophisticated statistical adjustment may not fully account for these confounders. One meta-analysis noted that controlling for socioeconomic status substantially diminished apparent hormone therapy effects [PMID 11113299].

Fourth, recall bias may have inflated effects in some epidemiological studies. Women with dementia and their caregivers may inaccurately remember past hormone use, and physicians may prescribe differently to women showing early cognitive changes [PMID 11113299].

Fifth, most research has examined women ages 60 and older, leaving a knowledge gap for women in their 40s and 50s who typically initiate hormone therapy for symptom management. The critical window hypothesis cannot be adequately tested without studies that begin treatment at menopause onset and follow women for decades—a practical challenge given the time and cost involved.

Finally, stroke risk associated with hormone therapy complicates the cognitive picture. Some evidence suggests estrogen increases stroke incidence in postmenopausal women, and strokes profoundly impact cognitive function [PMID 20001955]. This introduces a potential pathway for cognitive harm not directly related to estrogen's effects on neurons.

## Bottom line

After decades of research encompassing observational studies, small trials, and large randomized controlled trials, the evidence does not support using menopause hormone therapy to prevent cognitive decline or dementia in postmenopausal women. While estrogen demonstrates neuroprotective properties in laboratory settings and the biological rationale remains compelling, these benefits do not translate into clinically meaningful cognitive improvements for most women.

Short-term hormone therapy for managing menopausal symptoms appears to have minimal cognitive impact—neither substantially helpful nor harmful. However, longer-term use (beyond several years) and combined estrogen-progestagen formulations may carry cognitive risks that should be weighed against benefits for symptom control.

Women with premature ovarian insufficiency or surgical menopause before age 40 may represent an exception, potentially benefiting from hormone replacement initiated near the time of menopause and continued until the typical age of natural menopause, though more research is needed to confirm this.

Current clinical guidance appropriately recommends that hormone therapy decisions be based on symptom severity, quality of life impact, cardiovascular risk factors, and other health considerations—not cognitive protection. For women and their healthcare providers navigating menopause management, cognitive outcomes should not drive prescribing decisions in either direction.

The search for interventions to preserve cognitive function during aging continues. Hormone therapy, despite early promise, has not proven to be that intervention for most women. Future research may identify specific subgroups, formulations, or treatment windows that offer benefit, but until such evidence emerges, expectations should remain modest.
